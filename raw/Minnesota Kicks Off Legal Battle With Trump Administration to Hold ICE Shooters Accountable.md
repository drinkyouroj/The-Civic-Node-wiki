# Minnesota's Lawsuit Against Trump Administration Over ICE Shootings

## Legal Action Filed

In late March 2026, prosecutors from Hennepin County and the state of Minnesota filed a federal lawsuit against the Departments of Homeland Security and Justice. Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty characterized the action as "unprecedented in American history."

## The Three Shootings Under Investigation

The lawsuit centers on evidence related to three incidents during Operation Metro Surge (December 2025–early 2026):

1. **Renee Good** (killed January 7) — A 37-year-old mother of three shot by an ICE agent
2. **Julio Cesar Sosa-Celis** (wounded January 14) — A Venezuelan immigrant shot a week after Good
3. **Alex Pretti** (killed January 24) — Shot by federal agents

## Withheld Evidence and Cooperation Breakdown

Prosecutors sought extensive evidence including:
- Weapons used in all three shootings
- Official investigative reports
- Agent GPS data
- Witness statements

Initially, federal officials agreed to participate in a joint investigation with Minnesota's Bureau of Criminal Apprehension after Good's shooting. However, after public statements by Trump administration officials, the federal government reversed course and refused cooperation.

The Trump administration has not released the agents' names, though the Minnesota Star Tribune and ProPublica independently identified those involved in the Good and Pretti incidents.

## Legal Mechanisms and Demands

Prosecutors issued formal "Touhy letters" (demand letters requesting federal evidence when the government isn't a party) with a February 17 deadline. On February 13, the FBI notified state investigators it would not share materials in the Pretti case. More than a month after the deadline, no materials had been provided.

Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison stated: "The federal government has refused to cooperate with state law enforcement, which is unique, rare and simply cannot be tolerated."

## Major Legal Obstacles

**Supremacy Clause Defense**: If charges are filed, the primary hurdle is proving agents don't qualify for immunity under the Constitution's supremacy clause—a doctrine protecting federal officers from state prosecution if acting lawfully within their duties. Legal experts noted this presents steep challenges.

Jill Hasday, University of Minnesota constitutional law professor, observed: "That depends on the facts, but probably the odds are stacked against it."

The U.S. Supreme Court has not addressed supremacy clause immunity cases in over 100 years.

## Procedural Complications

- **Leadership transition**: Moriarty is not seeking reelection and leaves office end-of-year; her successor could inherit prosecutions
- **Agent location unknown**: Prosecutors lack knowledge of where agents currently reside
- **Extradition challenges**: Minnesota may need cooperation from other states to extradite agents, which law professor Ilya Somin characterized as "sort of iffy"
- **Potential defiance**: Professor Timothy Johnson noted that even if prosecutors win the lawsuit, compliance is uncertain: "The answer is probably no."

## Broader Investigations

Moriarty's office has opened 14 additional criminal investigations into potentially unlawful federal agent conduct during Operation Metro Surge, including:
- Excessive force allegations
- Incidents at a high school involving force against staff and students
- Probe into Gregory Bovino, Border Patrol commander who was videotaped dispersing chemical agents at Minneapolis protests

The Department of Homeland Security claimed agents responded to a "hostile crowd."

## Official Positions

- **DHS**: Defended Good's shooting as self-defense; said Pretti case under investigation by FBI and DHS with potential discipline or civil rights charges
- **Justice Department**: Did not respond to comment requests; neither agency has responded to the lawsuit
- **Moriarty**: Called the lawsuit "critically important" but stated she has not decided whether to file charges
