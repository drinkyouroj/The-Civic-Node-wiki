Iran is pushing back on parts of a tentative deal being negotiated by the Trump administration that could put an end to the war between the two countries.

President Trump on Saturday evening announced the two sides were moving close to a deal. Iranian officials have acknowledged the talks, and have even said there has been progress. But they are also suggesting major sticking points could prevent a final deal from being reached.

The Fars news agency reported Sunday that the agreement would allow Iran to manage the Strait of Hormuz, through which 20 percent of the world’s oil supply flows. Those shipments have mostly been halted since the war began in March, causing oil and gas prices to skyrocket around the world.

Trump on Saturday said the deal would lead to the Strait of Hormuz being opened, but did not mention Iranian management of the waterway. The Fars news agency, which is linked to Iran’s government, said Trump’s remarks about the Strait of Hormuz were [“inconsistent with reality.”](https://x.com/Osinttechnical/status/2058299205429375013)

Fars said that under the terms of the deal, Iran would allow the number of ships passing through the Strait to return to “pre-war” levels, but that this would not “in any way mean ‘free passage’” through the Strait, as Trump had contended.

President Trump is under enormous pressure to relieve high gas prices in the United States by opening the Strait. Average gas prices in the nation are above $4.50 per gallon, and have ramped-up worries within the Republican Party about this fall’s midterm elections, where the GOP is seeking to protect its majorities in the House and Senate.

Fars reported that the management of the Strait “would continue to be a monopoly” run by Iran. It also said that Iran had made no commitments on its nuclear program as a result of the talks.

Preventing Iran from obtaining nuclear weapons has been a key part of Trump’s argument for the war, and [GOP criticism](https://thehill.com/homenews/senate/5892991-gop-lawmakers-pressure-trump-iran/) on Saturday about the emerging deal centered on the argument that the U.S. was not getting enough commitments from Tehran to justify the war.

“The rumored 60-day ceasefire — with the belief that Iran will ever engage in good faith — would be a disaster,” Sen. [Roger Wicker](https://thehill.com/people/roger-wicker/) (R-Miss.), the chairman of the Senate Armed Services Committee, wrote in a post on [social platform X](https://x.com/SenatorWicker/status/2058227973644324915?s=20).

Sens. [Ted Cruz](https://thehill.com/people/ted-cruz/) (R-Texas) and [Lindsey Graham](https://thehill.com/people/lindsey-graham/) (R-S.C.) also publicly raised doubts about the wisdom of the deal.

Iran has seen its economy be battered by the war, and is seeking a deal that would end the fighting, its state media reported. But it appears to be insisting on retaining its sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz, which has given it leverage with the United States in the end game of the nearly three-month war.

The terms of how shipping traffic will be handled through the Strait is a major sticking point to reaching a final deal.
