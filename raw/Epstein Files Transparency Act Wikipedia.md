# Epstein Files Transparency Act

Wikipedia entry documenting H.R. 4405, the Epstein Files Transparency Act, which was passed by the 119th Congress and signed into law by President Donald Trump on November 19, 2025.

## Passage and Signing

- House vote: **427–1**, November 18, 2025.
- Senate: passed by unanimous consent, November 19, 2025.
- Signed into law: November 19, 2025 by President Trump.
- Primary House sponsors included Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Rep. Ro Khanna (D-CA).

## Statutory Requirements

The law required the U.S. Attorney General to make publicly available all files pertaining to the prosecution of Jeffrey Epstein and associated investigations within **30 days of passage** — by December 19, 2025. The law required release of all unclassified documents, with limited, specified exceptions for victim identification and ongoing investigative information.

## Enforcement Mechanism — Critical Note

**The law did not establish a penalty for noncompliance** with the December 19 deadline. The Act contains no provision for fines, civil liability, contempt, or any enforcement mechanism against DOJ officials who fail to meet the disclosure requirements.

This absence of enforcement teeth is central to the law's operational history: when the DOJ missed the December 19 deadline and made redactions the Act explicitly prohibited, there was no legal consequence available. Congressional oversight was limited to a request for an Inspector General audit.

## December 19 Release

According to CBS News reporting, the December 19 release contained **approximately 3,965 files totaling 3 GB of data**. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche characterized this as a partial release, promising "several hundred thousand" files that day with "several hundred thousand more" over subsequent weeks.

The DOJ acknowledged it had not released all unclassified Epstein files as the statute required.

## Total File Estimates

Estimates of the full universe of Epstein-related documents held by the federal government have grown substantially since the law's passage:

- **November 2025**: Unreleased documents consisted of "nearly 100,000 pages" plus 40 computers, 70 CDs, 26 storage drives, and six recording devices containing 300 GB of data collectively.
- **Late December 2025**: The DOJ announced it had discovered "over one million" additional potentially responsive documents, requiring "a few more weeks" to process. The New York Times reported DOJ was reviewing "as many as 5.2 million files."
- **January 2026**: Rep. Ro Khanna stated the Justice Department had "identified over 6 million potentially responsive pages."

## Release Timeline After Deadline

- **January 30, 2026**: Fifth release of documents; Deputy AG Blanche claimed this fulfilled the DOJ's legal obligations. Congressional critics, including members of the law's Republican sponsorship, disputed this claim.
- By early January 2026, approximately 40,000 pages had been released in total across multiple batches — less than 1% of the reviewed universe and a small fraction of the 6 million potentially responsive pages.

## Congressional and Oversight Response

**Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY)**, one of the law's chief Republican authors, posted publicly on X after the deadline: **"DOJ did break the law by making illegal redactions and by missing the deadline."**

**Twelve senators — Republican Lisa Murkowski and eleven Democrats** — sent a letter to Acting Inspector General Don Berthiaume on December 24, 2025, stating that victims "deserve full disclosure" and "the peace of mind of an independent audit" of DOJ's compliance with the Act.

**Deputy AG Todd Blanche** told NBC's *Meet the Press*: the review continues "simply to protect victims," and critics demanding faster release "apparently don't want us to protect victims."

## Pam Bondi / Kash Patel Correspondence (February 2025)

Attorney General Pam Bondi wrote to FBI Director Kash Patel on February 27, 2025 — her first full day in office — demanding "the full and complete Epstein files" by 8 a.m. February 28. In the letter, Bondi stated that before Patel took office she had received approximately 200 pages of documents (primarily flight logs, a contact list, and a victims' names and phone number list). She further stated that she had learned from a source that the FBI Field Office in New York was in possession of thousands of pages of documents related to the investigation.

Bondi's letter demonstrated that the DOJ sought files on her first day. The subsequent 10-month delay in public release occurred under an administration that had demanded the files from Day 1 — undercutting any argument that the timeline is attributable to ordinary review complexity.
