Those on the right have said they will use KOSA to target queer and trans content. So why do some Democrats support it?

![Image may contain Adult Person Electronics Phone Mobile Phone and Photography](https://media.them.us/photos/6690392998664afd78e57355/1:1/w_960,c_limit/kosa-1.jpg)

Photo via Getty Images

Pride Month is over. As the “LOVE IS LOVE” banners come down and companies lose the rainbow gradients from their logos, we’re faced with a painful truth: LGBTQ+ people, especially the most marginalized among us, are in the crosshairs of a queerphobic backlash that is targeting our health, our histories, and especially our youth. And things are getting worse, not better.

According to NPR, [half of all US states](https://www.npr.org/sections/shots-health-news/2024/07/03/nx-s1-4986385/trans-kids-health-bans-gender-affirming-care) now ban gender-affirming care for people under 18. [Eight states](https://www.lgbtmap.org/equality-maps/curricular_laws) now censor LGBTQ+ issues from school curricula via “ [Don’t Say Gay](https://www.them.us/story/new-settlement-rolls-back-parts-of-dont-say-gay-law-in-florida-schools) ” laws, and [two](https://www.njleg.state.nj.us/bill-search/2024/A670) [more](http://www.oklegislature.gov/BillInfo.aspx?Bill=HB%202546&Session=2400) states are considering similar legislation this year. The number-one book [targeted for censorship](https://www.them.us/story/book-bans-conservative-advocacy-groups) is a graphic novel [memoir about gender identity](https://www.them.us/story/conservative-lawsuits-young-adult-novels-gender-queer-a-court-of-mist-and-fury).

This June, Democratic lawmakers marched in Pride parades and spoke on stages, vowing to protect our community and fight back against legislative attacks on queer youth. [But some of](https://www.seacoastonline.com/picture-gallery/news/local/2024/06/22/portsmouth-pride-march-2024/74181712007/) [these](https://www.upi.com/News_Photos/view/upi/85169a107aef2324c18109f0897563b1/2024-NYC-Pride-March-Parade-in-New-York/) [same](https://www.instagram.com/elizabethwarren/p/C8AIqekOifv/?img_index=1) [lawmakers](https://www.washingtonblade.com/2024/07/02/parades-community-events-held-to-mark-pride-month-in-va/) are actively pushing federal legislation that would cut LGBTQ+ youth off from resources, information, and communities that can save their lives. Currently, [38 Democratic senators](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/1409/cosponsors) support the Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA), a bill that is vocally opposed by many queer and trans youth, along with a coalition of human rights and LGBTQ+ groups.

As a queer- and trans-led advocacy group focused on the ways technology impacts human rights, our organization, [Fight for the Future](https://www.fightforthefuture.org/), has seen bills like KOSA before: misguided internet bills that try to solve real problems, but ultimately throw marginalized people under the bus by expanding censorship and surveillance rather than addressing corporate abuses. KOSA’s most obvious predecessor is SESTA/FOSTA, a Trump-era bill that its supporters claimed would clamp down on online sex trafficking. Instead, the bill did [almost nothing](https://newrepublic.com/article/162823/sex-trafficking-sex-work-sesta-fosta) to accomplish its goal, and has [actively harmed LGBTQ+ people and sex workers](https://www.vice.com/en/article/a3aj3k/congress-attacked-sex-workers-now-we-need-you-to-stand-with-us) whose harm-reduction resources were decimated by the subsequent crackdown on online speech.

Like SESTA/FOSTA, some of KOSA’s supporters have positive intent. Many lawmakers and organizations support KOSA because they are concerned about real harms caused by Big Tech, like addictive design features and manipulative algorithms. But, also like SESTA/FOSTA, KOSA doesn’t touch the core issues with Big Tech’s extractive, exploitative business model. Instead, KOSA relies on a “duty of care” model that will pressure social platforms to suppress any speech the government is willing to argue makes kids “depressed” or “anxious.”

Under KOSA, platforms could be sued for recommending a potentially depression- or anxiety-inducing video to anyone under 18. [We know from past experience](https://www.vox.com/culture/2018/4/13/17172762/fosta-sesta-backpage-230-internet-freedom) that in order to protect their bottom line, social media companies will overcompensate and actively suppress posts and groups about gender identity, sexuality, abortion — anything they’re worried the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) could be willing to argue “harms” kids. How do you think a potential Trump administration’s FTC would use that kind of authority?

Other features of the bill stretch its censorship potential further. Despite language claiming that the bill does not require platforms to conduct “age verification,” to meaningfully comply with the law, platforms will have to know who is under 18. This means they’ll institute invasive age verification systems or age-gating, which can completely cut off access for LGBTQ+ youth who have unsupportive parents, and/or make it unsafe for queer people to access online resources anonymously.

KOSA creates powerful new ways for the government to interfere with online speech. For this reason, the bill is like catnip to extreme right-wing groups like the Heritage Foundation, the coordinators of [Project 2025](https://democracyforward.org/the-peoples-guide-to-project-2025/), who have [explicitly said](https://www.techdirt.com/2023/05/24/heritage-foundation-says-that-of-course-gop-will-use-kosa-to-censor-lgbtq-content/) they want to use it to target LGBTQ+ content. KOSA’s lead Republican sponsor, Marsha Blackburn, has also said in an interview she wants to use KOSA to protect minors [“from the transgender.”](https://www.them.us/story/kosa-senator-blackburn-censor-trans-content)

Queer youth have learned about KOSA on social media, notably in [fandom communities](https://techcrunch.com/2023/07/28/fan-fiction-writers-rally-fandoms-against-kosa-the-bill-purporting-to-protect-kids-online/?guccounter=1), and have [helped drive](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2024/02/01/online-safety-hearing-opposition/) hundreds of thousands of emails to members of Congress opposing KOSA. It’s already made a difference: there have been several rounds of changes to the bill, and some of them have [reduced the severity](https://19thnews.org/2024/03/why-some-lgbtq-groups-oppose-the-current-kids-online-safety-act/) of the threat the bill poses to LGBTQ+ content online. But the logic behind KOSA is still fundamentally flawed and dangerous — which is why experts have consistently said it would [make kids less safe](https://assets.pubpub.org/bujb2qf1/COSL-06.04-11717506843758.pdf), rather than more safe.

KOSA is rooted in the same bad thinking behind abstinence-only sex education, book bans, and drag bans. We protect kids by listening to them, empowering them, and arming them with knowledge and resources to navigate the world. Not by cutting them off from discussions about difficult subjects and subjecting them to censorship and surveillance.

The people who most need to listen to queer youth are the Democratic lawmakers who, this Pride Month, claimed they want to defend them. So here, in their own words, are some of the thoughts of LGBTQ youth about legislation like KOSA. I hope lawmakers will listen.

- Jay, 17: “I am a transgender person, and when I was a preteen, looking down the barrel of the gun of puberty, I was miserable. I didn’t know what was wrong I just knew I’d rather do anything else but go through puberty. The internet taught me what that was. They told me it was okay… The internet is a crucial source of information for preteens and beyond and you cannot take it away. You cannot take away their only realistically reachable source of information for what the close-minded or undereducated adults around them don't know.”
- Atticus, 16: “I would’ve never realized I was being sexually abused when I was 10-13 if it wasn’t for people sharing their experience with sexual abuse online. I gained a sense of community with other victims that I couldn’t obtain in real life.”
- Felix, 15: “Social media has improved my life in a lot of ways. My household is abusive. I am scared of my parents. I can’t leave my room, or I risk getting hurt. I risk losing food privileges. I am rarely allowed to interact with people outside of my family, excluding school days. Without social media, I wouldn’t have human connection. I wouldn’t have any joy.”
- Anonymous, 16: “Social media taught me to be curious. It taught me caution and trust and faith and that simply being me is enough. It brought me up where my parents failed, it allowed me to look into stories that assured me I am not alone where I am now. I would be fucking dead right now if it weren't for the stories of my fellow transgender folk out there, assuring me that it gets better.”

Many young people have [tried hard](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2024/02/01/online-safety-hearing-opposition/) to be heard on KOSA. They’ve called and emailed lawmakers, testified at hearings, and spoken up at protests and rallies. They’re [filming videos](https://www.badinternetbills.com/#testimonials), building [online communities](https://techcrunch.com/2023/07/28/fan-fiction-writers-rally-fandoms-against-kosa-the-bill-purporting-to-protect-kids-online/?guccounter=1), and [spamming comment sections](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6DSQtv97U4). Yet, aside from a few lawmakers — like Senator Ron Wyden, Representative Frank Pallone, and Representative Maxwell Frost, who have all acknowledged serious problems with the bill — too few Democrats are stepping up to stop KOSA’s passage.

A few months ago, it seemed almost certain that KOSA would pass. With the clock running out on this Congressional session and the bill still mired in controversy, it now seems [much more likely](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2024/06/28/house-privacy-talks-implode-spectacular-fashion/) that the bill will fail to advance this year. But KOSA, and other misguided efforts, like age verification bills and bills targeting online expression, will keep coming back — until lawmakers listen to the young people who are most impacted.

We urgently need progressives in Congress to present [a liberatory vision](https://www.fightforthefuture.org/news/2024-07-01-weve-been-fighting-each-other-while-big-tech-gets-away-with-murder-to-win-liberatory-tech-policy-we-need-a-unified-strategy-and-a-clear-division-of-labor/) for how to address the harm of Big Tech companies, without throwing trans kids and human rights under the bus. We need strong privacy, transparency, antitrust, civil rights, and algorithmic justice legislation that reduces the power of giant corporations rather than controlling what young people can and can’t see and say.

Queer youth are on the frontlines using the internet as a tool to fight for a better, safer world. The question is, do Democrats in Congress have their back?
