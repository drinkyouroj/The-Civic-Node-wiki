Few could have predicted that China would one day disrupt global supply chains with bureaucratic red tape. Yet on 4 April 2025, Beijing unveiled [licensing requirements](https://english.mofcom.gov.cn/Policies/AnnouncementsOrders/art/2025/art_0dd87cbee7b045bf93fabe6ab2faceee.html) for exporters of seven rare earth elements and magnets, creating a backlog of applications as businesses clamoured to secure their supply lines. China has weaponised seemingly innocuous administrative processes to modulate the flow of rare earths to critical industries.

Asia Pacific manufacturers scrambled to submit licence applications and find [alternative suppliers](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/rare-earth-magnet-users-jolted-into-paying-premium-prices-ex-china-supply-2025-07-01/) amid soaring compliance costs. Though a Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson insisted the controls were ‘ [non-discriminatory](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/xw/fyrbt/202506/t20250605_11641768.html) ’, their introduction just two days after US President Donald Trump’s ‘Liberation Day’ tariffs left little doubt about Beijing’s [geopolitical intent](https://eastasiaforum.org/2025/09/12/chinas-diplomatic-influence-on-the-line-as-it-navigates-the-us-tariff-storm/).

On 9 October, China’s Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) escalated its export control regime. The measures expanded licensing requirements to [five additional rare earths](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_59ec4f6bec0b459aa4a30c4bbd0a41c1.html) and [related processing technologies](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_1315078cebe04210bc35c72a4e7f7967.html), extended controls to [advanced semiconductor inputs](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_7fc9bff0fb4546ecb02f66ee77d0e5f6.html), [superhard materials](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_949f47563b834dad95b0010f375a892c.html) and [lithium-battery components](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_79646f0161564975a938fe00fee158d5.html) and imposed restrictions on [military end-use](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_7fc9bff0fb4546ecb02f66ee77d0e5f6.html). Most consequentially, it [introduced extraterritorial oversight](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_7fc9bff0fb4546ecb02f66ee77d0e5f6.html) of re-exports and end-use abroad — a major step in the evolution of its rare earth export controls.

The [s](https://cms-lawnow.com/en/ealerts/2025/10/china-tightens-export-controls-on-rare-earths-takeaways-for-global-businesses) [taggered rollout of MOFCOM notices](https://cms-lawnow.com/en/ealerts/2025/10/china-tightens-export-controls-on-rare-earths-takeaways-for-global-businesses) suggested that Beijing had been pacing implementation to align with trade negotiations. After the Trump–Xi meeting on 30 October, China agreed to a [one-year suspension](http://www.news.cn/world/20251030/dd3984dcc50b4c13bab9d2849da6e1b3/c.html) of the latest rare earth controls. Yet the breadth and complexity of the rules still demonstrate Beijing’s capacity to use administrative levers as geopolitical tools.

The April disruption offers a glimpse of what may lie ahead if the paused controls are enforced more broadly. Exports of rare earth magnets to South Korea and Japan [collapsed by 93 and 91 per cent](https://www.bloomberg.com/toaster/v2/charts/mpogwha261n4ndhstyf862dmo22tulo7.html?web=true&amp;variant=light&amp;logo=true&amp;titles=false&amp;interactive=true&amp;padding=false&amp;footer=true&amp;hideLogo=false&amp;hideTitles=true&amp;noPadding=true%22%20scrolling=%22no%22%20title=%22Toaster%20Chart%22%20width=%22100%25%22%20style=%22height:293px;width:618px;border-bottom-color:rgb\(0,%200,%200\);border-bottom-style:none;border-bottom-width:0px;border-image-outset:0;border-image-repeat:stretch;border-image-slice:100%25;border-image-source:none;border-image-width:1;border-left-color:rgb\(0,%200,%200\);border-left-style:none;border-left-width:0px;border-right-color:rgb\(0,%200,%200\);border-right-style:none;border-right-width:0px;border-top-color:rgb\(0,%200,%200\);border-top-style:none;border-top-width:0px;box-sizing:border-box;display:block;vertical-align:middle;%22%20%3E%3Cdiv%20class=%22html1%22%20style=%22position:relative;z-index:0;overflow-y:hidden;overflow-x:hidden;width:100%25;height:100%25;min-height:293.0px;text-align:initial;background-color:rgb\(255,%20255,%20255\);background-image:none;background-repeat:repeat%20repeat;background-position:0%25%200%25;background-size:auto;%22%3E%3Cdiv%20class=%22html%22%20lang=%22en%22%20style=%22overflow-x:visible;overflow-y:visible;-webkit-locale:&quot;en&quot;;margin-bottom:0px;margin-top:0px;padding-bottom:0px;padding-left:0px;padding-right:0px;padding-top:0px;%22%20%3E%3Clink%20id=%22toaster-stylesheet-mpogwha261n4ndhstyf862dmo22tulo7) between March and May, respectively. By May, China’s magnet exports were roughly $60 million — the [lowest monthly figure](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-06-20/china-s-rare-earth-magnet-exports-slump-in-may-especially-to-us) since data began in 2015, pandemic months aside. Whether the delays were [unintended](https://asia.nikkei.com/spotlight/supply-chain/how-red-tape-amplified-china-s-rare-earth-disruptions) or [by design](https://merics.org/en/comment/beijings-rare-earths-export-licensing-system-delays-design), China’s ‘ [licensing wall](https://www.defenseone.com/ideas/2025/07/how-chinas-new-rare-earth-export-controls-target-pentagonand-world/406606/) ’ gave it control over who received supplies and when.

Two mechanisms were central. The first was procedural — exporters were bound by a ‘ [one batch, one licence](https://edition.cnn.com/2025/05/20/business/china-rare-earth-export-controls-analysis-intl-hnk) ’ rule. This required a permit for each shipment and extensive documentation on product specifications, end-use, workforce profiles and [visual evidence](https://www.ft.com/content/0fce7177-a713-4c06-ba22-0ae429efe73f) of facilities. Chinese dual-use regulations stated that processing times could take up to [45 working days](https://finance.sina.com.cn/roll/2025-05-22/doc-inexkzvk7230378.shtml). But one rare earth trader observed they could [stretch for months](https://www.reuters.com/markets/commodities/chinas-rare-earth-exports-grind-halt-trade-war-controls-bite-2025-04-11/). The second was physical — customs inspections added friction, with shipments delayed for [chemical testing](https://www.ft.com/content/13d18620-d3d8-417e-b7fb-40d97fc064bf) or product-code verification — even for products outside the controls.

For manufacturers, these chokepoints disrupted schedules, raised compliance costs and forced a frantic search for alternatives. In India, shortages pushed Maruti Suzuki to [slash production](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/indias-maruti-suzuki-cuts-near-term-ev-production-amid-rare-earths-crisis-2025-06-10/) of its first EV by two-thirds, while its Japanese parent company Suzuki temporarily [halted the Swift](https://www.reuters.com/markets/commodities/suzuki-motor-halted-swift-car-production-due-chinas-rare-earth-curb-nikkei-says-2025-06-05/) line. In South Korea, [diminishing samarium supplies](https://www.reuters.com/markets/commodities/suzuki-motor-halted-swift-car-production-due-chinas-rare-earth-curb-nikkei-says-2025-06-05/) threatened the production of samarium-cobalt magnets used in defence and aerospace systems. Some, like Hyundai Motor, coped by [stockpiling during brief relaxations](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/hyundai-motor-has-rare-earths-stockpile-that-can-last-about-year-source-says-2025-06-10/), but that insurance has narrowed. Beijing has [warned foreign firms](https://www.ft.com/content/9f9e222d-f351-4e0f-be9b-aab309562c6c) that hoarding rare earths or magnets will invite scrutiny and tighter quotas, particularly when orders deviate from historical patterns.

China’s crackdown on stockpiling and stricter enforcement entrenches dependence and preserves its quasi-monopoly over rare earths. The approach echoes Xi Jinping’s 2020 call to tighten global industries’ dependence on Chinese supply chains as [strategic leverage](https://www.gov.cn/xinwen/2020-10/31/content_5556349.htm). The licensing regime operationalises that vision and the October measures push it even further.

Any product manufactured abroad [containing at least](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_7fc9bff0fb4546ecb02f66ee77d0e5f6.html) 0.1 per cent Chinese-origin rare earth materials by value — or produced using Chinese rare earth technologies — now requires MOFCOM approval before re-export to a third country. Applications from foreign military users also face denial, as do companies on Beijing’s watch lists and their majority-owned subsidiaries. The [50 per cent equity threshold](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_7fc9bff0fb4546ecb02f66ee77d0e5f6.html) means a parent company’s blacklisting can cascade through a corporate family, blocking access for ostensibly civilian operations. End-uses involving advanced semiconductors or artificial intelligence with [potential military applications](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_7fc9bff0fb4546ecb02f66ee77d0e5f6.html) will face case-by-case scrutiny.

China’s expanded export regulations mean that manufacturers face a constant risk calculus. The granular firm-level data provided to state authorities grants Beijing a [comprehensive view](https://files.libcom.org/files/Seeing%20Like%20a%20State%20-%20James%20C.%20Scott.pdf) of operations, dependencies and vulnerabilities. Enforcement of the extraterritorial provisions remain uncertain, but the government has already developed a [panopticon-like](https://direct.mit.edu/isec/article/44/1/42/12237/Weaponized-Interdependence-How-Global-Economic) vantage point within the global industrial network.

Diversifying away from China will be costly and slow. While some firms are [redesigning products](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2025/06/23/companies/honda-supplier-daido-steel/) or investing in parallel supply chains, the October controls now [extend to recycling operations](https://www.reuters.com/markets/commodities/recycling-pioneers-race-close-rare-earths-scrap-gap-2025-09-12/). Once enforced, export licenses would be required wherever materials are processed using Chinese rare earth [recycling technologies](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zwgk/zcfb/art/2025/art_6cb42957741440c6984de696b70df9ae.html), although this provision is currently paused.

Alternative production hubs are [slowly emerging](https://eastasiaforum.org/2025/10/28/breaking-beijings-rare-earths-advantage/). The US-backed MP Materials is establishing a domestic ‘ [mine-to-magnet](https://www.mining.com/mp-materials-lands-multi-billion-pentagon-deal/) ’ supply chain. Australia’s Lynas is expanding [heavy rare earth processing](https://wcsecure.weblink.com.au/pdf/LYC/02957737.pdf) outside China. Canberra plans to launch its [Critical Minerals Strategic Reserve](https://www.pmc.gov.au/domestic-policy/critical-minerals-strategic-reserve) by 2026 and has signed a [critical minerals deal](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/historic-critical-minerals-framework-signed-president-trump-and-prime-minister-albanese) with Washington to build secure supply chains.

For manufacturers reliant on rare earths, China’s licensing regime is no longer a temporary irritant. With enforcement tightening and timing aligned to diplomatic events, firms must treat conditional access and ongoing surveillance as permanent realities and [build resilience](https://sinolytics.de/global-business-news/blog/public/uploads/article/pdf/2/Whitepaper_Export-Controls.pdf) through exposure mapping, strategic buffers and alternative sourcing.

The April disruption proved that administrative friction works. October’s escalation made it a fixture of global manufacturing and demonstrated how bureaucracy is increasingly a tool of economic statecraft.
