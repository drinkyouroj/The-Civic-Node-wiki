Like many of you, I’ve watched as our state has become the center of national news. I wanted to cover this story differently than other outlets, in way that allows you investigate the data on your own terms.

To do this, I’ve spent the last week poring over the federal government’s official narrative: the press releases, the social media posts and the “Worst of the Worst” registry meant to showcase high-profile arrests. While many outlets have either ignored these reports or taken them at face value, I painstakingly gathered every available data point to understand not just what is being said, but what is missing.

The result is an interactive map of “on the record” ICE arrests in Minnesota, categorized by the claims the government has made about them. A streamlined tool where you can see what the federal government is sharing about who ICE is arresting where you live. To be clear, I have not verified each of their criminal histories with court records, rather am including the “official” claims from the federal government about these individuals.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0wOwBBGyRPg)

Here is a walkthrough of how to use the map, along with the clues I found in the data.

Before you look at the map, it is critical to understand what you are seeing.

Department of Homeland Security officials state they have made over [**3,000 arrests**](https://www.dhs.gov/news/2026/01/19/ice-continues-remove-worst-worst-minneapolis-streets-dhs-law-enforcement-marks-3000) during “Operation Metro Surge” in the last six weeks. However, across the public registry and press releases, I could only identify **335 names**.

When I asked DHS about this discrepancy, a spokesperson responded:

> *“There is a massive trove of criminal data, and we are working to expand the scope and size of the website to bring maximum transparency to the American people. DHS’s Worst of the Worst website only lists a fraction of the hundreds of thousands criminal illegal aliens arrested since President Trump took office. This project is ongoing, and the website will be updated regularly.”*

**This means the map below represents approximately 11% of the total operation.** It is a snapshot of what the government has chosen to reveal, not the entirety of what has occurred.

**Two notes on accuracy:**

- **Randomized Locations:** For arrests that included a location, DHS often provided only a city name. To visualize the scale of the operation, I randomized the dots within the vicinity of the arrest city.
- **Missing Arrest Location Data:** No arrest location was given for **87 individuals** (approx. 26% of the data), who were simply identified as being captured in “Operation Metro Surge.” Because they have no geographic data, those names do not appear on the map, though their charges are included in the analysis below.

I manually re-categorized every arrest based on a standard that matters to our community, rather than bureaucratic jargon. While many detainees faced multiple charges, I classified them by the most serious offense listed.

- 🔴 **Violent & Sexual Offenses (Red):** This includes homicide, sexual offenses, assault, and robbery. If someone hurt a person or a child, they are marked in red. This was the largest group in the released names, representing **190 of the 335 names (57%)**.
- 🔵 **Felony Re-Entry (Blue):** This is a federal border crime. It is illegal and serious, but distinct from physical violence against a neighbor. There were **34 arrests** in this category.
- 🟡 **Drug Trafficking vs. Possession (Gold/Grey):** I made a distinction between someone caught with a baggie in their pocket (Grey) and someone moving weight (Gold). There were **42 trafficking arrests** compared to only a handful of possession charges.
- 🟢 **Property & Fraud (Teal/Silver):** These mark crimes that damage our community through larceny or DUI. Fraud made up the smallest category of all, with only **14 names** listed.

The operation is branded as “Metro Surge,” implying a sweep of Minneapolis streets. The data released tells a more complicated story.

The “Prison Pipeline” (Rural Areas) (The Watch)

When you look at the map, you’ll notice a trend: small, rural towns have a disproportionately large number of arrests.

- **Sandstone & Rochester:** These towns accounted for **71 arrests**. Why? Likely because they host major federal prisons (FCI Sandstone and FMC Rochester).
- **Waseca:** Nearly all ICE arrests in this town were women. This corresponds directly to the Federal Correctional Institution in Waseca, which houses female inmates.
- **Superior, WI:** While ICE hasn’t commented specifically on the Twin Ports, two names appeared in Superior’s “Worst of the Worst” registry. This highlights the role of the Douglas County Jail, which frequently acts as a holding facility for federal detainees in the Duluth-Superior area.

**The Takeaway:** In rural Minnesota, ICE appears to be taking credit for administrative transfers of inmates who were already in custody.

The “Metro Reality” (Twin Cities) (The Watch)

However, the pattern shifts when we look at the Twin Cities. Unlike the administrative transfers seen in rural prison towns, the dots in Minneapolis and St. Paul are scattered, reflecting enforcement operations happening in neighborhoods.

When we combine these verified Metro arrests with the “Ghost Data” (names DHS released without a location), we get a dataset of **174 individuals**. This gives us the clearest picture of who DHS is telling us they’re arresting in communities.

- **124** of these individuals are flagged for **Violent & Sexual Offenses** (71% of the group).
- This includes **59** individuals accused of sex crimes and **16** accused of homicide.

**The Takeaway:** While the rural numbers may be inflated by prison transfers, ICE is arresting *some* people in metro communities with significant criminal histories.

**The lingering question is: who** **are the thousands of other detainees we** ***don’t*** **know about?**
