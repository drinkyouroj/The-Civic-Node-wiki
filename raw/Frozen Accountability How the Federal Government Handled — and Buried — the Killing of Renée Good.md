The federal government's response to the January 7, 2026, killing of Renée Good by ICE agent Jonathan Ross did not begin with an investigation. It began with a defense — and what followed was a methodical institutional effort to ensure that defense was never seriously tested.

---

## The Shot Through the Windshield

Shortly after 9 a.m. on a Wednesday in south Minneapolis, Renée Nicole Good, a 37-year-old U.S. citizen and mother of three, was driving on Portland Avenue when her vehicle encountered a large federal immigration enforcement detail operating under *Operation Metro Surge*, a Trump administration dragnet targeting the Twin Cities.¹ Good's wife, Becca Ganger, was a passenger. Witnesses later said the couple had stopped to observe the operation.²

What happened in the next few seconds was captured from multiple camera angles — by bystanders, by federal agents' own body-worn devices, and by witnesses who had gathered as ICE and partner agencies worked the block.³ Good reversed her vehicle and turned the steering wheel to the right. As the car began moving forward, agent Jonathan Ross fired three rounds through the windshield.⁴ One struck her arm. One struck her right breast. The third entered the left side of her head.⁵ The head wound was fatal. Paramedics arrived at 9:42 a.m. and found Good unresponsive in the driver's seat, her wife holding her, both of them covered in blood.⁶

Good was unarmed. She had no criminal record connected to any violent offense. She had just dropped her six-year-old child at school.⁷

DHS Secretary Kristi Noem went public within hours. Good had "weaponized her vehicle," Noem said, and Ross had acted in self-defense. She said he "did exactly what he was taught to do" — a statement issued before any investigation had been conducted and before any evidence had been publicly released.⁸ A subsequent New York Times analysis of bystander video contradicted the Trump administration's claim that Good had "viciously run over" an agent.⁹

## Who Is Jonathan Ross?

Jonathan Ross, 43, grew up in Indiana and enlisted in the Army National Guard in 2002.¹⁰ He deployed to Iraq from November 2004 through November 2005 as a machine gunner on a gun truck with the Indiana Guard and earned the Army Commendation Medal.¹¹ After his return, he attended college and joined U.S. Border Patrol near El Paso, Texas, in 2007, where he worked as a field intelligence agent focused on drug cartels and human trafficking.¹²

Ross joined ICE in 2015 as a deportation officer in Minnesota.¹³ By the time of the Good shooting he had accumulated one of the most operationally dense profiles in the Minneapolis field office: team leader for fugitive operations, where he focused on "higher value targets"; SWAT team member; member of the FBI's Joint Terrorism Task Force; and *both* a firearms instructor and an active-shooter instructor.¹⁴ In court testimony last December, he explained his work in clinical terms: "I develop the targets, create a target package, conduct surveillance, and then devise a plan to execute the arrest warrant."¹⁵

That December trial arose from an incident on June 17, 2025. While participating in the arrest of Roberto Carlos Muñoz-Guatemala, a Mexican national with a prior conviction for criminal sexual conduct, Ross broke open the rear window of Muñoz-Guatemala's vehicle and reached inside to unlock the door.¹⁶ Muñoz-Guatemala accelerated, dragging Ross approximately 300 feet — weaving back and forth, in the government's words, "in an apparent attempt to shake" the officer from the car.¹⁷ Ross twice fired his Taser during the drag. He was seriously injured. Muñoz-Guatemala was convicted of assaulting a federal officer.¹⁸

That conviction, and its ongoing post-conviction proceedings, have become the legal vehicle through which a federal judge ordered, on April 8–9, 2026, the disclosure of materials central to understanding what happened on January 7.¹⁹ The court's order — issued because Muñoz-Guatemala argued Ross's post-incident record was relevant to sentencing and potential grounds for a new trial — required production of Ross's *complete* personnel and training files, all use-of-force policies in effect on both June 17, 2025, and January 7, 2026, all of Ross's statements in the sixty minutes before Good's shooting, any statements made by federal witnesses to the Good shooting, cell phone data from Ross's device on January 7, drug and alcohol test results, psychological fitness-for-duty evaluations, and all government video from thirty minutes before through sixty minutes after the shooting.²⁰ The deadline: **May 1, 2026**.²¹

## The Protocol That Was Supposed to Apply

The use of deadly force by a federal officer is not a matter of discretion left to the moment. It is governed by a layered and specific legal framework that exists precisely because the stakes are irreversible.

At the constitutional foundation sit two Supreme Court decisions. *Tennessee v. Garner* (1985) held that deadly force against a fleeing suspect is unconstitutional unless the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious bodily injury.²² *Graham v. Connor* (1989) established the "objectively reasonable" standard under the Fourth Amendment — requiring courts to weigh the severity of the crime, the immediacy of the threat, and whether the suspect was resisting or merely fleeing, all from the vantage point of a reasonable officer at the moment of the act.²³

DOJ Policy 1-16.000 translates those constitutional floors into federal operational rules: deadly force is permitted only where an officer has a *reasonable belief* of imminent danger of death or serious bodily injury to themselves or others.²⁴ The DHS Use of Force Directive of 2018 adds a mandatory sequencing requirement: an agent involved in a shooting must be placed on administrative leave for a minimum of three days, extended pending all investigative proceedings, and ICE's Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR) *may not begin its own independent disciplinary review until after the external investigation has concluded*.²⁵

ICE's own internal use-of-force training is even more specific on one point: agents are taught *not to position themselves in front of a moving vehicle* and *not to fire at a moving vehicle*, on the explicit ground that shots will not stop the vehicle and will create additional danger.²⁶ That training prohibition sits at the center of the Good case — because whether Ross violated it is precisely the question the May 1 disclosure order was designed to answer.²⁷

The mandatory post-shooting protocol also requires immediate reporting, a joint investigation involving the appropriate primary law enforcement agency, and — as a matter of foundational professional standards — that an officer who has caused a death should remain off street enforcement until all proceedings are resolved.²⁸

## Day One: The Evidence Lockout

On the morning of January 7, 2026, after consultation among the Hennepin County Attorney's Office, the U.S. Attorney's Office, and the FBI, it was agreed that the Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension (BCA) and the FBI would conduct a *joint investigation* into Good's death.²⁹ That agreement lasted hours.

By evening, the FBI had reversed course. BCA investigators were told they would no longer have access to the crime scene, to case materials, to scene evidence, or to investigative interviews — the elements, the BCA said in a formal statement, "necessary to complete a thorough and independent investigation."³⁰ BCA Superintendent Drew Evans announced on January 8 that his agency had "reluctantly withdrawn" from the probe, stating that the FBI was "restricting evidence."³¹ Evans said his agency remained committed to a full review "if and when" the FBI reconsiders — an eventuality that, as of April 29, 2026, has not occurred.³²

The physical evidence has remained similarly locked down. Good's Honda Pilot has been held shrink-wrapped by federal authorities since January 7, 2026.³³ In late April 2026, Good's widow, Becca Ganger, filed a motion arguing that because the federal government has repeatedly said it will not prosecute Ross, its continued retention of the SUV "prevents any meaningful inquiry into Good's killing."³⁴ The Hennepin County Medical Examiner's official autopsy has also been withheld and not publicly released.³⁵ The family's independent autopsy, commissioned through the law firm Romanucci & Blandin, confirmed three entry wounds — arm, right breast, left temple — with the head wound fatal.³⁶

## No Civil Rights Case — and Then: Investigate the Widow

Within approximately one week of the shooting, DOJ Deputy Attorney General Emil Bove announced publicly that the government had found "no basis" for a civil-rights investigation of Ross.³⁷ Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights Harmeet Dhillon separately informed her division's staff that no investigation would proceed.³⁸

What DOJ senior officials directed *instead* was more striking: prosecutors in the Minnesota U.S. Attorney's Office received pressure from Main Justice to open a *criminal investigation into Becca Ganger*, Good's widow, based on her alleged connections to activist organizations that monitor immigration enforcement.³⁹

The response from career federal prosecutors was swift and unambiguous. On January 13, 2026, Acting U.S. Attorney Joseph H. Thompson — who had led the significant *Feeding Our Future* federal fraud prosecution — resigned, along with five additional Assistant U.S. Attorneys including Melinda Williams, Harry Jacobs, and Thomas Calhoun-Lopez.⁴⁰ Separately, at least four senior leaders of the DOJ Civil Rights Division's Criminal Section in Washington also departed amid the turmoil.⁴¹

BCA Superintendent Evans did not disguise his assessment of the resignations: "We're losing a true public servant," he said of Thompson, adding that the absence of a credible investigation "will undermine trust in our public safety agencies."⁴² The six resignations in a single day from one U.S. Attorney's office over a single case have no clear modern precedent in the history of federal prosecution in Minnesota.⁴³

---

## The FBI Probe and the Mergen Controversy

The FBI's internal fracture did not stop at the crime scene. According to reporting based on senior DHS officials and whistleblower accounts submitted to Senate Judiciary Committee Democrats — including Senators Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI) and Dick Durbin (D-IL) — Minneapolis FBI Public Corruption Squad supervisor Tracee Mergen departed the bureau after being pressured to *reclassify* her civil-rights inquiry into Ross as an investigation of Good herself, on the theory that Good had assaulted a federal officer.⁴⁴

The whistleblower accounts go further. According to those disclosures, FBI Director Kash Patel ordered forensic experts to stand down from processing the shooting scene, specifically because he "did not want Good referenced as a 'victim' in the warrant."⁴⁵ The Senate Judiciary Democrats described Patel as wanting "to falsely spin Renée Good as a threat to law enforcement" by characterizing her as the *subject* of an officer-assault investigation rather than the victim of a use-of-force incident.⁴⁶

These are serious allegations and they deserve precise characterization: they are based on named-source congressional submissions and investigative reporting, and they have been widely covered. *They have not been confirmed by court documents, an Inspector General report, or on-the-record official statements, and the FBI has not publicly confirmed or denied the specific substance — stating only that it is "FBI policy not to comment on personnel matters."* ⁴⁷ Mergen's resignation itself, however, is confirmed by multiple outlets.⁴⁸

The structural consequence of this impasse is not merely an abstraction. Under the DHS Use of Force Directive, ICE OPR *cannot* open its own disciplinary review of Ross until the FBI formally closes its case.⁴⁹ The FBI has not closed its case. Jonathan Ross therefore faces no formal accountability process — internal or external — of any kind.⁵⁰ He has, according to reporting from March 2026, been reassigned to active immigration enforcement duty in another state.⁵¹

## Minnesota Fights Back

Minnesota has not accepted the federal posture of unilateral evidence control without a legal response. On January 12, 2026, Attorney General Keith Ellison filed suit against DHS in federal court, alleging that Operation Metro Surge violated the First Amendment, the Tenth Amendment, and the Administrative Procedure Act — specifically that it targeted sanctuary jurisdictions for their political views.⁵² An amended complaint filed in April 2026 incorporated survey data showing extreme racial bias in DHS enforcement actions, and economic estimates that Minneapolis and Saint Paul residents lost over $240 million in wages and businesses lost over $600 million in revenue during the operation.⁵³

On March 24 (approximately), 2026, three government plaintiffs joined forces in a second action: the State of Minnesota, Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty, and the BCA sued both DOJ and DHS, seeking court-compelled disclosure of evidence related to the Good, Pretti, and Sosa-Celis shootings.⁵⁴ Moriarty was direct: "One of the sovereign states of this nation is suing for evidence regarding the deaths of two of its residents and the shooting of another by federal agents within its jurisdiction; evidence that is intentionally being withheld by the federal government."⁵⁵ Ellison called it extraordinary that the suit was necessary at all, noting that cooperation between federal and state law enforcement had been entirely routine in Minnesota for decades.⁵⁶ Legal observers characterized the conflict as, in ProPublica's framing, "a game of constitutional chicken: states' rights versus federal supremacy."⁵⁷

The federal government's response to Minnesota's escalating legal pressure included a move that officials and advocates have characterized as retaliatory: on or around January 20, 2026, DOJ served grand jury subpoenas on six Minnesota state and local offices — including those of Governor Tim Walz, AG Ellison, and Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey — as part of a criminal investigation into whether their public statements opposing ICE tactics constituted obstruction of, or conspiracy against, federal law enforcement.⁵⁸ Mayor Frey publicly released his subpoena; it demanded records going back to January 2025 concerning any "lack of cooperation" with federal authorities.⁵⁹ Governor Walz called it "a bullying tactic meant to quell political opposition."⁶⁰

And then, on April 8–9, 2026, a federal judge in the Muñoz-Guatemala proceeding issued the evidence-turnover order described above — the most specific judicial command yet issued in any proceeding touching the Good case — with a May 1, 2026, deadline.⁶¹ Whether the government complies, seeks a stay, or defies the order will be the next significant development in a case that has already reshaped the relationship between Minnesota and the federal government, consumed a U.S. Attorney's office, driven out an FBI supervisor, and left a mother's death — documented on video, confirmed by a private autopsy — without a single officer being suspended, charged, or formally disciplined.⁶²
