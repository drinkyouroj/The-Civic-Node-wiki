As members of Emory University’s Class of 2030 walked through campus on April 10 for [Atlanta Campus Preview Day,](https://news.emory.edu/stories/2026/02/er_campus_preview_day_traffic_alert_26_02_2026/story.html) approximately 80 community members participated in a walkout to protest the Flock Safety cameras on campus. The DeFlock Emory Coalition, a student and faculty coalition consisting of campus groups such as EmoryUnite!, Emory National Lawyers Guild, Emory Students for Justice in Palestine, Emory’s chapter of American Association of University Professors (AAUP), Sunrise Emory and Emory Students for Socialism (SFS), promoted the [walkout](https://www.instagram.com/p/DWh09eujyNy/).

[Flock Safety](https://www.flocksafety.com/) is a security and technology company that partners with local, state and federal governments to provide artificial intelligence (AI)-powered services such as automated license plate recognition.

Several cities across the United States have ended their [partnerships](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/17/nx-s1-5612825/flock-contracts-canceled-immigration-survillance-concerns) with Flock amid concerns that the company could share collected data with federal agencies like Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE).

According to DeFlock Emory, at least seven Flock [cameras](https://www.instagram.com/p/DWh09eujyNy/) are present on Emory’s campus. Assistant Vice President of University Communications Laura Diamond confirmed the existence of the Flock cameras on Emory’s campus in a statement to The Emory Wheel. Diamond mentioned that only sworn EPD officers have access to the footage acquired from Flock cameras across campus.

“Flock cameras have been on Emory’s Atlanta campus since 2024,” Diamond wrote. “These cameras are license plate readers used collaboratively by law enforcement agencies to aid in crime investigations. For example, the Emory Police Department uses the cameras to investigate threat cases and criminal trespass issues.”

Diamond directed the Wheel toward a Jan. 6 [statement](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/does-flock-share-data-with-ice) from Flock denying any partnership with ICE. The company stated that any access to Flock data by ICE would need to be explicitly granted by the customer and “comply with applicable law.”

“Emory does not share footage with the federal government unless law enforcement officials present a valid criminal warrant or specific court order issued by a federal judge,” an Emory University [spokesperson](https://www.atlantanewsfirst.com/2026/04/11/emory-students-speak-out-against-license-plate-readers-campus/) told Atlanta News First.

During the walkout, protestors gathered in Asbury Circle before marching to the Emory University Administration Building to deliver a [petition](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSeuiiPBDwGDJ74Yz55MPqVzHubAYEKVn7HliRdmWYUAkqVzKQ/viewform?fbclid=PAZXh0bgNhZW0CMTEAc3J0YwZhcHBfaWQMMjU2MjgxMDQwNTU4AAGnUrUnmSoDJlMw_9MFF-TeCy36kfwj_JZCCiRpg2soGeFrJYbPnrWBdWBGqRw_aem_a7WUOLuLmWBm2aVZS7ZrWA) calling on the University to end its relationship with Flock Safety, remove all license plate reading cameras from campus property and create a community-led review of campus surveillance practices. According to members of DeFlock Emory, the petition has reached almost 1000 signatures as of April 10. EmoryUnite! also launched a “Student Workers Against ICE!” campaign at the protest and they are giving the University 10 days to respond to the petition and student activism.

SFS member Amaris Christian (29C) discussed the impact of surveillance on members of the University community.

“This surveillance is not useful,” Christian said. “Remember that police do not need a warrant to track people and block technology, track all vehicles, all faces, all activities.”

She also highlighted the lack of communication between the University and students about the use of these technologies.

“This administration is hoping that we won’t speak out or search into the real reason we have these cameras, and so we demand that Emory give us information on what data is being collected, who has access to it, and how it’s truly being used,” Christian said.

With Flock’s technology dependent on AI, Sunrise Emory Co-Founder Maya Vizuete (28C) raised concerns about increased nationwide surveillance and its environmental impacts. According to Consumer Reports, one AI data center can use up to 5 million [gallons](https://www.consumerreports.org/data-centers/ai-data-centers-impact-on-electric-bills-water-and-more-a1040338678/#:~:text=In%20the%20process%2C%20a%20data,by%20the%2025%2Dyear%20megadrought.) of water every day for cooling.

“Emory has a choice to make,” Vizuete said. “It can continue expanding surveillance infrastructure that places environmental costs on communities across Georgia or pursue transparency, accountability and community safety instead.”

Other members of SFS, SunriseEmory, EmoryUnite! and AAUP also shared their worries about the Flock cameras on campus, emphasizing their impact on community spirit. For Robert Birdwell, professional tutor and developmental editor at the Emory Writing Center, the University’s statement that the Emory Police Department does not share data with ICE is not enough.

“We cannot accept any assurances at face value, that there is some barrier between ICE and the police,” Birdwell said.

Birdwell also called on the University to support “vulnerable” immigrant and international students and faculty against potential federal government intervention.

Attendee Nishi Kamani (29C) said the presence of Flock cameras on Emory’s campus has negatively impacted immigrant students. Kalani attended the event to learn more about the controversy surrounding Emory’s Flock camera system and to participate in the protest.

“Administration should definitely take this protest into account and consider making changes and removing them \[Flock cameras\] from campus, especially with the petition that’s being worked on and this protest,” Kamani said.
