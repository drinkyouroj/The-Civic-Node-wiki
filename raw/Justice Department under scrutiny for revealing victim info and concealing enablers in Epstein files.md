# Justice Department Scrutinized Over Epstein Files Redaction Handling

**Core Issues**

The DOJ faced criticism for inconsistent redaction practices when releasing over 3 million Epstein documents on January 30, 2026. The department failed to properly obscure victim identities while simultaneously redacting names of individuals who allegedly aided the convicted sex offender.

**Victim Information Exposure**

Multiple survivors discovered their names, addresses, and phone numbers in the released files. Survivor Dani Bensky stated: "To have pieces of my life be out there on display...was really troublesome." The DOJ subsequently reached an agreement with survivors' lawyers to avoid a federal court hearing after identifying nearly 100 victims whose information was improperly disclosed.

**Concealed Enabler Information**

CNN's review identified redacted individuals appearing to facilitate Epstein's activities. In one 2015 email, a redacted person wrote to Epstein: "And this one is (i think) totally your girl." Another 2014 message from a redacted sender stated: "Thank you for a fun night…Your littlest girl was a little naughty."

The controversial draft indictment from the 2000s listed three co-conspirators described as conspiring to "persuade, induce, and entice" minors into prostitution—all names redacted.

**Withheld Content Statistics**

The DOJ withheld approximately 200,000 pages citing "deliberative process privilege, the work-product doctrine, and attorney-client privilege." Total released: 3.5 million pages of approximately 6 million identified as potentially responsive under the Epstein Files Transparency Act (signed Nov 19, 2025).

**Bipartisan Congressional Response**

Democratic Representative Ro Khanna and Republican Representative Thomas Massie, who championed the Epstein Files Transparency Act, demanded access to unredacted materials. Khanna stated: "The DOJ has protected the Epstein class with blanket redactions in some areas while failing to protect the identities of survivors in other areas."

Rep. Jamie Raskin (top Democrat, House Judiciary) said the department appeared to have violated the law by concealing names and passages that did not meet the statute's narrow redaction standards.

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer: "Simply releasing a mountain of blacked out pages violates the spirit of transparency and the letter of the law."

Massie: The release "grossly fails to comply with both the spirit and the letter of the law."

**Survivor Statements**

Survivor Jess Michaels pointed to heavily redacted FBI forms, noting that four of seven pages were blacked out describing her experience and perpetrators. She emphasized the contradiction: "If there's no men, then there's no reason to redact it."

Survivor Sharlene Rochard characterized the response as systemic failure: "Publishing images of victims while shielding predators is just a failure of complete justice."
