In April 2026, the most consequential monetary policy event in the United States wasn’t a rate decision. American [gasoline prices rose about 37%](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/trump-says-us-will-target-irans-infrastructure-tuesday-2026-04-05/) in five weeks, from under $3 a gallon to $4.11. The Federal Open Market Committee voted nothing, moved nothing, and had no instrument that could have changed the outcome.

The cause wasn’t demand. It wasn’t money supply. It was a waterway 21 miles wide at its narrowest point, controlled by a country the United States has been at war with since February 28.

The Federal Reserve’s dual mandate covers maximum employment and price stability. It has never covered reopening a shipping lane. That’s always been true. April 2026 is the most expensive demonstration of it. The [Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2026/4/28/when-will-strait-of-hormuz-be-safe-for-commercial-shipping-again) as you read this, with about 5% of pre-war traffic moving. Kevin Warsh is about to inherit a Federal Reserve facing 3.3% inflation, two concurrent supply shocks rate policy can’t address, and a confirmation record that says tariffs won’t cause inflation to spike.

### The Spark: the rate decision nobody voted on

About 20% of the world’s oil normally moves through the Strait. Right now, [20,000 mariners are stranded on cargo ships](https://www.euronews.com/2026/04/27/strait-of-hormuz-standoff-leaves-20000-seafarers-stranded-on-cargo-ships) in the Persian Gulf, waiting on a deal that hasn’t come. Traffic for the entire week of April 13-19 was about 80 vessels, down from 130-plus a day before the war. The IMO Secretary General’s flat assessment: “There is no safe transit anywhere in the Strait of Hormuz.”

That’s the upstream. Downstream is the gas pump.

Brent crude crossed [$100 a barrel by April 22](https://apnews.com/article/us-iran-war-hormuz-israel-pakistan-ceasefire-april-22-2026-267230f7f32b436822484479313840f7) after Iran’s IRGC fired on three ships and seized two of them. The European Energy Commissioner put the EU’s daily cost at about €500 million. Inflation in the US sits at [3.3% as of April 2026](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/04/25/dont-count-on-rate-cuts-just-yet-warsh-as-fed-chair-may-not-lead-to-big-policy-changes/), well above the Fed’s 2% target. The federal funds rate has been parked at 3.50%-3.75% for months.

What the Fed actually did during this: nothing. There was no emergency meeting on the Strait. There was no vote. The committee that holds the mandate for US price stability did not, in any operational sense, hold the lever for this particular inflation.

That’s not a failure of judgment. It’s a categorical mismatch between what the Fed’s instrument is built for and what’s currently moving prices.

Stalled at the chokepoint. The FOMC has nothing to say about it.

### The Pattern: the tool that doesn’t fit

The federal funds rate addresses two things well: demand-pull inflation and inflation expectations. Too much money chasing too few goods, or workers and firms baking expected price increases into wages and contracts. Rate hikes work on the demand side of the equation, by slowing the economy until demand cools.

Supply-shock inflation is a different animal. The cause is physical scarcity, not excess demand. You can’t raise interest rates to reopen a shipping lane.

Powell has said the quiet part out loud, just about a different supply shock. [“Essentially all inflation forecasts for the United States went up materially as a consequence of the tariffs”](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/economy/trumps-tariffs-kept-fed-cutting-rates-jerome-powell-says-rcna216220), he said in 2025. He was talking about Trump’s import taxes, but the structure is identical. The Fed can wait. It can hold. It can absorb the political pressure of doing neither.

History helps here, but only if you read it correctly. The 1973 Arab oil embargo is the textbook supply shock. OPEC cut output, [oil prices spiked roughly 400%](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/great-inflation), and the 1970s stagflation era arrived. Volcker’s response, a decade later, took the federal funds rate to [about 22% in mid-1981](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DFF). That broke inflation expectations and crushed demand into the 1981-82 recession. It did not break the oil shock. Oil prices fell in the early 1980s for separate reasons: new supply, demand destruction, weakened OPEC discipline. Volcker won the inflation war by winning the demand-side battle, while the supply-side problem resolved on its own.

Nobody is doing 22% rates against the Strait of Hormuz.

There’s a second piece worth pulling out, because it changes the shape of the problem. Iran isn’t just blocking the lane. It’s monetizing access. Per [Bloomberg’s primary reporting](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-24/iran-charges-some-ships-hormuz-transit-fees-for-safe-passage), Iran started charging up to $2 million per ship for safe passage in late March. By April 1, the system [hardened into a structured IRGC permit regime](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-01/strait-of-hormuz-ships-paying-iran-yuan-and-crypto-tolls-for-safe-passage): contact an intermediary, submit ship data, pass a security screen excluding Israel and US ties, pay in Chinese yuan or stablecoins, get a permit code, get a naval escort.

The yuan-and-stablecoin part is the upgrade. Every transit fee is denominated explicitly off-dollar. The Strait is now both a chokepoint and a small, persistent dedollarization vector for oil-linked trade. Iran is collecting a tax in a currency the FOMC does not set the rate for, on a flow the FOMC does not control, in a venue the FOMC has no jurisdiction over.

The Fed is downstream. The Strait is upstream. The committee adjusts the cost of money. It does not adjust the cost of oil when the cost of oil is set by a country with a chokepoint.

#### two shocks, one committee

The Strait shock didn’t arrive on a clean field. It landed on top of an existing supply-shock problem the Fed was already failing to solve.

Tariffs are the other one. The US effective tariff rate sits at [19.7% on a post-substitution basis, the highest since 1933](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/state-us-tariffs-july-14-2025) and the Smoot-Hawley era. That’s the rate that actually shows up in consumer prices once firms and shoppers reroute purchases. Powell’s “essentially all inflation forecasts went up” line was about exactly this. The cause is executive action. The fix is executive action. The president who created this inflation can remove it by signing an order. The Fed cannot.

The Strait shock works differently. Iran’s closure was triggered by US-Israeli strikes that began February 28. The resolution path runs through diplomacy, military de-escalation, or indefinite disruption. Even if Iran’s [10-point proposal](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/28/trump-reviews-iranian-proposal-aimed-at-reopening-strait-of-hormuz) and the US framework converge next week, the operational reopening of the Strait still takes about [6 months for US mine clearance](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2026/4/28/when-will-strait-of-hormuz-be-safe-for-commercial-shipping-again). And insurers won’t price the route at normal rates until they see sustained traffic, not isolated transits. War-risk premiums could rise from a pre-war 0.25% of hull value to about 5%, a 20x increase that gets priced into every barrel of oil that does move.

So the FOMC has one rate-setting lever and two concurrent supply shocks with different political origins, different resolution paths, and the same property: rate policy can’t address either.

The historical playbook doesn’t offer a clean comp. Nixon didn’t cause OPEC. The 1951 Truman-Fed standoff was a wartime fiscal president fighting the central bank, but Truman wasn’t also generating the supply shock. In 2026, the same president pressuring the Fed to cut rates is also the source of one of the two supply shocks holding inflation up. There is no historical chair-and-environment combination that maps to this.

The committee inheriting that environment isn’t the committee that designed it. Kevin Warsh is about to walk in.

### The Protocol: what Warsh inherits

Warsh’s confirmation arc is the architecture that constrains him before his first meeting.

He spent the entire process declining to publicly engage with the two largest inflation drivers facing the Fed. He’s argued [tariffs won’t cause inflation to spike](https://www.cfr.org/articles/why-kevin-warsh-wont-revolutionize-the-federal-reserve), aligning with Trump’s position. He took no public stance on the Powell DOJ probe, the dominant institutional pressure on the Fed during the same period. The Senate Banking Committee voted [13-11 on April 29](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/29/trump-fed-nominee-kevin-warsh-senate-approval.html) to advance him. Strict party line. The first fully partisan committee vote on a Fed Chair nominee in the committee’s history.

The full Senate floor vote is expected the week of May 11. Powell agreed to remain until Warsh is confirmed. As I’m writing this, Warsh isn’t confirmed yet. He’s also not really in doubt: Republicans hold 53 seats, Sen. John Fetterman has signaled he plans to vote yes, and the floor math is straightforward.

Two pieces of structural reality limit what Warsh can do once he gets there.

First, he holds [one of twelve FOMC votes](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/04/25/dont-count-on-rate-cuts-just-yet-warsh-as-fed-chair-may-not-lead-to-big-policy-changes/). The committee that just voted 11-1 to hold rates in March will still be the committee. He chairs it. He doesn’t dictate it.

Second, his credibility comes pre-encumbered. Jon Faust, a former Powell adviser, put it this way: “Warsh comes in with essentially none of the gravitas that Greenspan had. Instead, Warsh comes in with the baggage that Trump has really loaded on him.” Wall Street is currently pricing minimal probability of rate cuts before October 2027. Treasury Secretary Bessent’s [April 25 comment](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/04/25/dont-count-on-rate-cuts-just-yet-warsh-as-fed-chair-may-not-lead-to-big-policy-changes/) (”if the Fed wanted to wait for some clarity before cutting rates, I understand that”) got read by Fed-watchers as cover for holding, not a push to cut.

The bind is a real one. If Warsh holds, he has to explain why, and an honest explanation names the Strait and the tariffs as the binding constraints. That’s politically uncomfortable for a chair confirmed by Trump, on tariffs that Trump owns. If he cuts, he cuts into 3.3% inflation and two active supply shocks. The 11 voting members who don’t answer to him just voted to hold.

His first FOMC statement as chair is the tell. It will either name the supply-shock environment accurately, or it will obscure it. If you’re reading the post-meeting statement and you see no mention of either tariffs or the Strait as binding constraints, that’s the answer. The chair just told you what kind of chair he’s going to be.

### The Personal Code: what this is actually about

The American consumer’s cost of living isn’t set by one institution. It’s co-determined by three: the Federal Reserve, the executive branch (through tariffs), and any foreign actor controlling a critical chokepoint (right now, Iran). Of those three, only the Fed is structured for political independence. And that independence is itself under institutional strain. The other two are explicitly political.

Iran’s $2M-per-ship transit fee, denominated in yuan and stablecoins, will keep running regardless of the next FOMC decision. The 19.7% effective tariff rate will keep running regardless of the next FOMC decision. The Fed’s tools are calibrated for one class of inflation. May 2026 is delivering two others on top.

The question Wall Street keeps asking is whether the Fed will cut rates. That’s the wrong question. The right one is whether the chair can describe the environment honestly, given that his confirmation required him not to.

When Warsh’s first FOMC statement comes out, read it for what’s missing. The absence is the actual signal.
