### Section 2 — Analysis: What a "New Accord" Actually Would Be

Word target: ~600 words | Tone: analytical, building, with one controlled landing on dollar-policy ambition
Argument job: Convert the abstract "coordination" framing into specific operational mechanics, anchor it in the 1951 Accord that it would partially undo, name the fiscal pressure that motivates it, and engage the FX-endgame interpretation honestly without overclaiming.

#### 2a. The 1951 Accord, briefly but accurately (~140 words)

**¶7 — What the Accord was, and what it ended.**

Facts to deploy:
- The [March 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/treasury-fed-accord) ended the Federal Reserve's wartime obligation to peg Treasury bond yields and restored the Fed's authority to set monetary policy independently of Treasury debt-management needs. Source: [[1951 Treasury-Fed Accord]] concept page.
- The Accord was won *over Truman's direct objection* — he summoned the entire FOMC to the White House (the only time a sitting president has done so) to demand the peg be held. The Fed refused. Source: [[1951 Treasury-Fed Accord]]
- Modern Fed independence is 75 years old, not constitutional. Before 1951 the Fed was, in operational terms, a debt-management arm of the Treasury. After 1951 it could raise rates without Treasury permission. Source: same.

Argument move: Ground the historical anchor without sentimentalizing it. Independence is an institutional achievement, not an inheritance. It was *won* by refusing a wartime fiscal president. Brief, factual, no melodrama.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names the Accord as a *refusal* — not a procedure, not a memo, but a decision by the Fed's leadership to decline a sitting president's instruction. Sets up ¶8 by establishing the baseline the Warsh proposal would partially undo.

#### 2b. What the Warsh proposal would do (~200 words)

**¶8 — Two operational commitments.**

Facts to deploy:
- Warsh's framework, per [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — Jin Low Substack - 2026-02-24]] structural analysis, has two operational commitments: (1) explicit forward Fed guidance on balance-sheet trajectory; (2) Treasury alignment of debt-issuance timing with Fed liquidity operations. Source: same.
- Krishna Guha (Evercore ISI): the proposal grants Treasury "veto power over quantitative tightening decisions." Source: [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — TradingKey - 2026-02-09]]
- Tim Duy (SGH Macro): the framework "resembles Yield Curve Control, explicitly connecting monetary policy to deficit financing rather than inflation management." Source: same.
- Deutsche Bank modeled the operational consequence: the Fed's portfolio shifts from short-term T-bills at less than 5% of holdings to roughly 55%. Source: same.

Argument move: This is the most operationally concrete paragraph in the piece. Take the language seriously and unpack the mechanics. The "coordination, not subordination" phrase is the marketing; the Treasury veto on QT is the policy. The Deutsche Bank portfolio projection is the consequence.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that converts the Deutsche Bank projection from a number into an institutional consequence. A 5%-to-55% portfolio shift isn't a tactical adjustment; it's a structural transformation of what the Fed *is*.

#### 2c. Why now: the $1 trillion problem (~140 words)

**¶9 — The fiscal pressure that explains the timing.**

Facts to deploy:
- Federal net interest costs hit $270.3 billion in Q1 FY2026 — about $1.08 trillion annualized on $38.56 trillion in total federal debt. Source: [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — Jin Low Substack - 2026-02-24]]
- The Fed balance sheet is at $6.6 trillion as of February 2026. Source: same.

Argument move: Convert the abstract "coordination" claim into a fiscal motive. Treasury wants the Fed coordinating balance-sheet decisions because debt service is now a $1T/yr problem and any increase in borrowing costs is politically intolerable. The math is the motive. The accord proposal removes Fed discretion to make Treasury's problem worse.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names the asymmetry — the Treasury has a fiscal problem the Fed currently has the discretion to make worse. The accord removes the discretion. Marcus reads this and the financial-sociology gear clicks.

#### 2d. The "Statecraft" layer — handle with care (~140 words)

**¶10 — The dollar-policy frame, hedged.**

Facts to deploy:
- The Bloomberg headline framed Warsh's Fed agenda as backing a "Bessent-Rubio Statecraft Agenda for the Dollar." Source: [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — Bloomberg - 2026-04-22]]
- Capital Flows Research called the unstated objective "an FX endgame" — coordinated dollar weakening to restore US trade competitiveness without losing reserve-currency status. Source: [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — Capital Flows Research - 2026-04-23]]
- Three independent desk reads (Evercore ISI, SGH Macro, Deutsche Bank) converge on similar interpretations. Source: composite from coordination cluster.
- AP wire (Christopher Rugaber) explicitly notes "no explicit coordination between Bessent, Miran, and Warsh is documented in the piece" — the mainstream institutional hedge. Source: [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — AP via ADN - 2026-04-25]]

Argument move: Engage the most analytically ambitious read of the proposal — managed dollar decline as the unstated objective — without asserting it as documented fact. The institutional facts are sufficient without the conspiracy. Hedge clearly: this is documented public alignment + convergent analyst interpretation, not documented coordination.

Key sentence to land: A sentence that holds both halves — the FX-endgame interpretation is *plausible enough that three independent desks reached for it*, but it remains interpretation, and the piece's case doesn't require it to be true. The institutional facts are damning without needing to allege intent.

### Section 3 — Implications: The Bessent Cover, the Hidden Vote, the Ratification

Word target: ~350 words | Tone: tightening, prosecutorial, building to one declarative landing
Argument job: Make explicit what Sections 1 and 2 have set up — Treasury is the missing protagonist of US monetary policy in 2026, the FOMC arithmetic doesn't constrain what's actually being negotiated, and the institutional ratification has already occurred at the rhetorical level.

#### ¶12 — Bessent's "I understand" as the operating manual

Facts to deploy:
- Bessent's April 25 line: "if the Fed wanted to wait for some clarity before cutting rates, I understand that." Source: [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — AP via ADN - 2026-04-25]]
- Wall Street has priced minimal probability of rate cuts before October 2027. Source: same.
- Jon Faust (former Powell adviser): "Warsh comes in with essentially none of the gravitas that Greenspan had. Instead, Warsh comes in with the baggage that Trump has really loaded on him." Source: same.

Argument move: Convert "I understand" from a polite remark into the operating manual it is. Treasury is establishing that Warsh can hold rates without it being read as caving to Trump pressure. That gives Warsh runway. Runway buys time to deliver the *balance-sheet* changes — which don't generate the same political optics as rate cuts but reshape the Fed's portfolio, its independence posture, and its operational coordination with Treasury debt management. The market is watching the wrong instrument.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names what Bessent's "I understand" actually purchases — credibility runway for Warsh to make the structural moves without being seen to make political ones. Marcus reads this and adds Bessent to his mental list of who actually holds the lever.

#### ¶13 — The hidden vote

Facts to deploy:
- Twelve people sit on the FOMC. Source: [[FOMC]] entity.
- The March 2026 vote was 11–1 to hold; Warsh inherits that committee. Source: [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — AP via ADN - 2026-04-25]]
- The balance sheet is a different category of decision: less granular, less publicly contested, less covered by the financial press, structurally negotiable with the Treasury Secretary outside the FOMC framework. Source: synthesis.

Argument move: Name the structural workaround. Twelve people sit on the FOMC. Warsh holds one vote. But under the proposed framework, the consequential coordination happens *upstream* of the vote — between the chair and the Treasury Secretary, on the balance-sheet trajectory. Treasury isn't on the FOMC. Under Warsh's proposal, Treasury doesn't *need* to be.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names the hidden vote — Treasury's effective participation in the consequential decision, without the institutional accountability of being a voting member. The thirteenth seat that doesn't appear on the org chart.

#### ¶14 — What gets ratified

Facts to deploy:
- The post-1951 definition of independence: the Fed's ability to make decisions the Treasury Secretary doesn't agree with. Source: [[1951 Treasury-Fed Accord]]
- The Warsh definition of "coordination": the Fed agreeing in advance not to. Source: synthesis from [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — Bloomberg - 2026-04-22]] and [[Bessent Miran Warsh Coordination — Jin Low Substack - 2026-02-24]]

Argument move: The most prosecutorial sentence in the piece. Pull no punches at the institutional-definitional level. Independence, post-1951, *is* the ability to make decisions the Treasury doesn't agree with. The Warsh framework, in its own terms, removes that ability *prospectively*, by agreement, voluntarily.

Key sentence to land: The single most quotable line in the piece. It must do three things: (1) name the post-1951 definition of independence in plain English, (2) name what the Warsh framework would substitute for it, (3) refuse to call the substitution anything other than what it is. Not coordination. Not modernization. Not "more coordinated." A reversal, written in language too careful to call itself one.
