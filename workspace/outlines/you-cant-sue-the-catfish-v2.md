## What changed from v1

V1 was written from the user's brief with no source ingestion. It assumed a structure in which Destiny was a structurally innocent reachable defendant being hunted because the real wrongdoer was in Oxford. The complaint (ECF 1) and the KiwiFarms drop metadata invalidate that framing in a useful way.

**Destiny is plausibly liable on the statute.** The complaint contains a contemporaneous written admission ("I'd had phone convos and stuff with this person they were fairly close to me"), a pre-announcement allegation (he told "Rose" in advance he'd send her a video of a "young" girl), pattern evidence with 15+ women, alleged evidence destruction one day after plaintiff's public announcement, and the doxxing of the real Rose.

**The structural argument survives and sharpens.** The article is no longer "the system hunts the wrong person." The article is: **the system routes civil liability to whoever can be served, and in this case that happens to also be someone liable. That is not the system working. That is the system's default behavior coinciding with a correct outcome, which it does some of the time, by accident.**

The catfisher — Solo — who stole a minor's identity, ran a year-long impersonation, threatened to kill her family when she tried to leave, sat on the video for two years, and dropped it "because he was bored" — generates zero civil consequences in proportion to that conduct. He is in Oxford. He uses a burner account. The drop landed on a KiwiFarms thread that had been running since 2017. The infrastructure was there. He just had to click submit.

## Section 2: The Source Code (~500 words)
*Tone: sustained analytical. This is the structural core. No hedging.*

The lawsuit names one defendant. Steven Bonnell II. Known as Destiny. Miami Beach. Reachable.

And here is what the complaint alleges against him, in his own writing:

- On the evening of the leak, Destiny texted the plaintiff: *"there's literally no excuse, I'd had phone convos and stuff with this person they were fairly close to me."* A contemporaneous admission that he sent the video and treated a never-met text-only catfish as "fairly close."
- The complaint alleges his own messages with the fake Rose include him telling her, in advance, that he would send her a video of a young woman. The share was not a moment of weakness. It was pre-announced.
- Fifteen women contacted the plaintiff after the leak saying they had received sexually explicit images of other women from Destiny without consent. Three say he deleted their messages after receiving a preservation demand.
- When the real Rose — the teenage identity-theft victim — became searchable online, Destiny organized a Discord group to find her and posted her name and home address on Twitter.
- One day after the plaintiff publicly announced she would sue, Destiny began deleting messages with her. He later admitted, publicly, to deleting messages with multiple accusers.

This is the person the lawsuit will hold accountable. It is likely to succeed. That is the reachable defendant. The statute under which this proceeds, 15 U.S.C. § 6851, is sound. The Florida cyber harassment statute is sound. The liability will attach.

And none of that is the story.

The story is that the person who did everything that generated the moment of public harm — who stole a minor's identity on an app marketed to children, who ran an impersonation operation for a year, who threatened to kill her family when she tried to leave, who sat on the video for two years, who posted it to a Lolcows thread "because he was bored," who framed the drop as a "white Christmas," who fabricated a consent story four days later that plaintiff's teenage identity-theft victim has now rebutted under sworn oath — that person is in Oxford, operating a burner account, using a name he has not verified is real.

He is not a party to the suit. He never will be. Plaintiff has filed a letter rogatory in the UK; that mechanism is real but will not produce usable evidence on trial-compatible timelines.

This is the Named Hypocrisy: the civil legal system is presented as a machine for assigning liability to the person who caused the harm. It is actually a machine for routing liability to the person who can be served on time. Some of the time, as here, those are the same person. Those are the cases the system gets credit for.

In this case, the reachable defendant's own conduct is plausibly culpable on the statute. It is not the system's credit that it landed in the right place. It is the system's default behavior coinciding with a correct outcome, which it does some of the time, by accident. The system would route identically if Destiny had done nothing wrong — because Solo would still be in Oxford, and there would still be no one else to serve.

## Section 3: The Upgrade (~400 words)
*Tone: prescriptive, compressed, no hedging. The fixes should feel mechanical.*

Three structural fixes. All three target the gap between reachable fault and actual fault. None is about Destiny's defense.

**Fix 1 — A third-party catfisher provision in NCII statutes.**
Not to protect the reachable defendant. To give the unreachable actor a cause of action targeting them. Civil and criminal liability against impersonators, triggered by the combination of identity theft and NCII distribution, with tolling for cases where the impersonator is discovered only after the victim has been sued. As written, 15 U.S.C. § 6851 has no operational concept of an intermediary who stole the consent narrative itself. It cannot reach a Solo. That is the gap.

**Fix 2 — A digital-harm carve-out in foreign civil cooperation.**
Plaintiff in Doe v. Bonnell has already filed a letter rogatory targeting the UK. The mechanism is real. It is also slow and evidentially fragile enough that the reachable defendant will absorb the full practical weight of the suit before the process produces anything usable. Extradition has carve-outs for specific offenses; foreign civil discovery does not. Digital identity abuse and cross-border NCII are discrete enough to warrant a streamlined civil MLAT analog. Without it, reachability stays a gradient, and the gradient has a steep slope near the reachable party.

**Fix 3 — Platform design liability proportionate to design choices.**
The KiwiFarms thread where the drop landed has been running since March 2017. It has a subforum category called "Lolcows." It had a seven-year prior on the target. The distribution infrastructure was already built; Solo did not design it; he dumped into it. Section 230 treats the forum as a neutral pipe. The pipe is not neutral. It is optimized for exactly the scaling that occurred. Liability that attaches to platform design choices — subforum architecture, amplification decisions, retention defaults — targets the actual substrate of the harm. Nothing else does.

Structural point: all three fixes share a logic. The current legal infrastructure assumes harm flows through identifiable, jurisdictionally-reachable, directly-culpable, one-at-a-time actors. When any of those assumptions breaks — foreign, anonymous, platform-mediated, impersonator-intermediated — the system does not stop. It defaults to the nearest available reachable target and calls that justice.

In the cases where the reachable target is actually culpable, the system's credibility survives. In the cases where they aren't, it still routes the same way.

## Section 4: Personal Code (~230 words) — LOCKED
*Tone: sardonic, grounded, tonal drop from analytical to first-person*

A few years ago, in a depression full of psychotic features, I became certain — with the full conviction of a brain doing its best work under impossible pressure — that a gang had hacked my wireless network and was communicating with me through blinking LEDs. The Caps Lock key. The indicator on an electric fly swatter. Every flicker was a signal. A voice taunted me: *you're still owned.*

I was wrong about the specifics. I was not wrong about the structure.

The thing my nervous system invented — that someone I couldn't locate was controlling every interaction I had with a screen, that a trusted device was the medium of the taunt — is the factual situation Destiny was actually in. A person in Oxford, operating under a borrowed name, was controlling every message. The intimacy was real. The recipient wasn't. The "trusted contact" was the taunter.

The difference between my experience and his is that when I came out of the episode, I got to discover the gang wasn't real. When Destiny came out of his, the gang was real, operating from a jurisdiction US civil process can't reach, and the only defendant the system could locate was him — who, to be fair, had plenty of his own reasons to be locatable.

I run a Paraloncloud node. My photos live on Google. My git lives on GitHub. I know exactly which flickers I don't control.

That is coincidence, not design.* A vocabulary upgrade: next time the reader sees a high-profile civil case where the "obvious" defendant is also the reachable one, they ask who the proximate cause actually was, and whether the system had any alternative to naming the person they did.
