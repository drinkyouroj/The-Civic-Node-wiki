### Section 1: The Definition — A 160-Year-Old Economist Predicted Your Browser Tab Problem (~300 words)
*Tone: clear, slightly irreverent, accessible*

- Open with the **browser tab analogy**: Faster internet was supposed to make browsing more efficient. Instead, you have 47 tabs open. Faster processors were supposed to make computers feel snappy. Instead, software bloated to fill the capacity. Every efficiency gain in your life has produced more consumption, not less. There's a name for this, and it's 160 years old.
- **William Stanley Jevons, 1865**: British economist watching coal engines get more efficient. His peers assumed England would use less coal. Jevons saw the opposite — efficient engines unlocked factories, trains, steamships. New applications consumed far more coal than the old ones saved. "It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption."
- **The thesis by paragraph 2**: Jevons paradox — the observation that efficiency gains can increase total consumption — is the single most important framework for understanding why AI isn't going to use less of anything. It's also the most misused concept in tech CEO investor calls. Here's how to tell when it's real and when it's spin.

### Section 2: The Mechanics — Coal, Highways, Refrigerators, and the One Question That Determines Everything (~600 words)
*Tone: precise, patient, building — this is where operational credibility is earned*

- **The mechanism in plain language**: Efficiency lowers the cost per unit of output. Lower cost makes the thing accessible to more people and more use cases. New demand can exceed the savings. The key variable is **elasticity** — how much does demand respond to lower prices?
- **Coal (1865)**: Efficient steam engines didn't just make existing factories cheaper — they made new factories, railroads, and steamships possible. Total coal consumption exploded. Demand was elastic because the industrial revolution was inventing new uses for energy faster than efficiency could reduce per-unit consumption.
- **Highways (modern)**: Adding lanes to reduce congestion is the classic infrastructure version. More capacity → lower time-cost of driving → more people drive → congestion returns. Transportation economists call it "induced demand." Same mechanism, different resource.
- **Refrigeration (David Owen, New Yorker 2010)**: More efficient fridges didn't reduce household energy consumption. They led to multiple fridges per household, mini-fridges in hotel rooms, cold supply chains for fresh produce shipped globally. The efficient fridge didn't replace the old fridge — it multiplied the concept of refrigeration.
- **The counterexample that breaks the pattern — agriculture**: Tractors made farmers enormously more productive. But food demand is **inelastic** — people don't eat 6x more when food gets 6x cheaper. Farming went from 40% of the US workforce to under 2%. Efficiency destroyed millions of jobs because demand couldn't grow fast enough to absorb the productivity gains.
- **The one question**: Is demand for the thing being made efficient elastic or inelastic? If elastic (people will use way more of it when it's cheaper), Jevons applies — efficiency increases consumption. If inelastic (people won't use much more regardless of price), efficiency destroys jobs and reduces consumption. **This single variable determines whether efficiency is a gift or a weapon.**
- **Video streaming as intuition pump**: Before modern compression, 360p video used significant bandwidth. Better codecs didn't reduce bandwidth consumption — people streamed more content, at higher resolutions, on more devices. Total bandwidth consumption exploded. Elastic demand.
- Sources: [[Why the AI world is suddenly obsessed with a 160-year-old economics paradox]], [[Jevons Paradox]], [[Big A — The Crisis Got Weirder (RAM Apocalypse Update)]]

### Section 3: The Applications — The $71 Billion Bluff That Proved Jevons Right (~400 words)
*Tone: forward-looking, analytical, connecting to topics Marcus already follows*

- **The DRAM crisis as live demonstration**: OpenAI signed non-binding letters of intent for 40% of global DRAM supply. The LOIs triggered a 171% price spike in contract DRAM. Then Google published TurboQuant — a compression technique that cut AI memory requirements by 6x. Memory stocks crashed 5-6%. DDR5 kits dropped $60-100. Jevons is dead, right?
- **Wrong**: Within days, the market stabilized. Analysts applied the Jevons framework: if AI needs 1/6th the memory per context window, companies will use 6x longer context windows and deploy more AI agents. The efficiency gain won't reduce demand — it'll expand AI's addressable market. Samsung and SK Hynix recovered because Wall Street understood Jevons better than the initial panic sellers.
- **The AI labor question** — aviation or agriculture?: Erik Brynjolfsson (Stanford) argues AI could trigger a Jevons effect for certain occupations. Jets made pilots more productive → air travel demand exploded → more pilots needed. But if AI-augmented work looks more like farming (the output has inelastic demand), efficiency will destroy jobs, not create them. The answer isn't universal — it's occupation-by-occupation, and the elasticity question is what determines whether your job is aviation or agriculture.
- **Who captures the surplus?**: The optimistic Jevons story for AI labor assumes workers benefit from productivity gains. But there's nothing in the paradox that guarantees this. Employers could capture all the surplus — same output, fewer workers, higher margins. Jevons tells you total demand may grow; it says nothing about who gets paid.
- **Nadella's spin**: After DeepSeek showed AI could be built cheaply, Satya Nadella cited Jevons paradox to reassure investors. The concept is real. The application was self-serving. Every tech CEO invoking Jevons is telling you demand will grow; none of them are telling you who will benefit.
- Sources: [[How Sam Altman's OpenAI may have caused the worst consumer hardware crisis]], [[AI DRAM Crisis]], [[Leverage Erasure Through Automation]]

### Section 4: The Human Element — My Hard Drive Is a Museum of Efficiency Gains (~250 words)
*Tone: personal, first-person, slightly wry — the analogy comes home*

- **Personal angle**: I have a 2TB SSD. It's full. Ten years ago I had a 256GB drive. It was also full. Twenty years ago I had a 40GB drive. Full. Every storage efficiency gain in my lifetime has produced more stuff, not more space. I am Jevons' coal engine, and the resource I'm consuming is my own attention.
- **The browser tab callback**: Close the analogy from the opener. The 47 tabs aren't a failure of willpower. They're Jevons paradox operating at the speed of thought. Faster loading → lower cost per tab → more tabs. The efficiency didn't free you. It found new things to consume.
- **The question to leave with**: The next time someone tells you a new AI breakthrough will reduce costs, ask the Jevons question: **Is demand for this elastic or inelastic?** If elastic, costs won't fall — usage will rise to consume the savings. If inelastic, the savings are real, and so are the job losses. There's no version of efficiency that's free. Jevons figured that out staring at coal engines in 1865. We're still learning it with every open tab.
