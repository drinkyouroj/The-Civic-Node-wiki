# Outline: The Bluff Is Over. The Price Isn't.

## Five independent forces are holding DDR5 at $400 — and not one of them is the demand that drove it there in the first place.

**Trigger:** Early Warning — five converging forces trapping memory prices at crisis levels
**Template:** Pattern Report (multiple signals organized around a thesis)
**Timeliness:** Samsung strike vote begins May 21 (5 weeks away); South Korean helium reserves estimated to run out June 2026 (2 months); both are ticking clocks
**Callbacks:** Article 1 closing line was "The letter was non-binding. The price isn't." — this headline echoes it directly.

---

### Opener (~200 words)
*Tone: analytical, setting up the puzzle*

- Link back to Article 1: "In 'The $71 Billion Bluff,' we covered how a non-binding letter of intent locked the memory market at crisis prices. That's the origin story. This is why it won't end."
- The thesis: five independent forces — each separately insufficient to explain $400 DDR5 — are converging simultaneously. No single force is the story; the convergence is.
- Name the five upfront (same order as the Article 1 teaser): a 160-year-old economic paradox, a helium shortage from the Strait of Hormuz, a Samsung labor revolt, a data center collapse that paradoxically isn't releasing supply, and two Chinese factories that won't be ready until 2028
- Framing: each force has a bear case that should be pushing prices down, and a bull case that's actually holding them up

### Force 1: Jevons Paradox (~250 words)
*Tone: intellectual — the most counterintuitive force and the analytical anchor*

- The bear case: Google's TurboQuant (March 25, 2026) compresses LLM memory needs by 6x. Samsung and SK Hynix stocks dropped 5-6%. Chinese stockpilers panicked. DDR5 kits fell $60-100 from peaks. Looks like the beginning of a crash.
- The bull case: William Stanley Jevons, 1865. More efficient steam engines didn't reduce coal consumption; they made coal-powered factories viable in more places. Historical proof: video compression didn't reduce bandwidth — we got 4K streaming on every device.
- The AI version: 6x less memory per context window = 6x longer context windows + more concurrent AI agents + applications that weren't viable before. Total demand increases.
- The evidence: contract prices between OEMs and manufacturers didn't budge. The correction was concentrated in the speculative retail layer (Shenzhen's Huaqiangbei market: 30%+ drops). Samsung and SK Hynix margins were untouched.
- Sources: [[DDR5 Retail Prices Pullback Amid Market Correction — TrendForce]], [[Chinese Memory Vendors Claiming to Be Doomed — WCCFTech]], [[Jevons Paradox]], Google Research Blog TurboQuant

### Force 2: Helium from the Strait of Hormuz (~250 words)
*Tone: geopolitical — where the DRAM crisis connects to the Iran conflict*

- The bear case: the Strait of Hormuz is an oil story, not a memory story. Why would a war in the Middle East affect your RAM?
- The bull case: Qatar's Ras Laffan Industrial City produces ~33% of global helium. Largely offline since Iranian strikes and Strait closure in early March 2026. South Korea imports ~65% of its helium from Qatar. Samsung + SK Hynix = 70% of global DRAM.
- Why helium matters: ultra-high-purity helium (6N or better) is irreplaceable in chip fabrication — wafer cooling, lithography, etching, CVD, EUV. Memory fabrication is particularly helium-intensive due to repeated high-heat etching for 3D stacking. There is no substitute.
- The clock: South Korean fab helium reserves estimated at ~6 months (through June 2026). Samsung's HeRS (Helium Reuse System) buys time but doesn't solve the problem. If reserves run out, Samsung/SK Hynix prioritize high-margin HBM for AI over consumer DDR5.
- LNG supply also disrupted by the same Strait closure — compound energy + process gas squeeze on the same factories
- Sources: [[Helium Crisis Tightens Grip On Global Chip Supply Chain]], wiki entity pages for Samsung, SK Hynix, Qatar

### Force 3: Samsung's Labor Revolt (~200 words)
*Tone: sardonic — the irony is rich*

- The setup: Samsung posted Q1 2026 operating profit of 57.2 trillion won ($37.92B) — 8.5x year-over-year. 95% from chips. The company is printing money.
- The grievance: 90,000 unionized workers are voting on an 18-day strike from May 21. They want a 7% base wage increase plus profit-sharing. The pay gap with rival SK Hynix has widened.
- The leverage: one of three companies controlling 90%+ of global memory. A strike could halt ~50% of Pyeongtaek semiconductor production — Samsung's primary fab.
- The talent drain: Tesla is actively poaching Samsung chip designers, adding pressure beyond the union action
- The irony: the crisis that's generating windfall profits for Samsung is also generating the labor unrest that could further constrain supply. The workers know exactly how much leverage they hold.
- Sources: [[Samsung workers' strike plan would disrupt chip supply]], [[Samsung Flags Eightfold Jump in Q1 Profit — Reuters]]

### Force 4: The Data Center Paradox (~200 words)
*Tone: dry, paradoxical — the counterintuitive force*

- The bear case: half of US data centers planned for 2026 are delayed or cancelled. Only 4GW of 12GW under construction. If the data center buildout is collapsing, shouldn't memory demand be falling too?
- The paradox: the demand collapse isn't releasing supply because the binding contracts are already signed. Apple, Amazon, Microsoft locked in multi-year deals during the panic (see Article 1). The data centers those contracts were supposed to fill may never be built, but the memory procurement is real and irrevocable.
- The bottleneck: the data center delays aren't even about memory — they're about electrical components (transformers, switchgear, batteries) from China, representing <10% of construction costs but gating entire projects
- The implication: the market can't self-correct. Normally, falling demand releases supply. Here, the demand contracted but the supply is contractually committed. The pressure valve is broken.
- Sources: [[Almost Half of US Data Centers Slated to Be Canceled or Delayed — Futurism]], [[Stargate Data Center Expansion Cancelled — Oracle and OpenAI]]

### Force 5: Chinese Factories Still Two Years Away (~200 words)
*Tone: forward-looking — the timeline for relief*

- CXMT: building a Shanghai mega-fab 2-3x larger than its Hefei headquarters. Equipment installation 2026, volume production targeting 2027. Also adding HBM3 lines for domestic AI accelerators. Revenue surged 130% YoY; filed IPO for $4.1B.
- YMTC: constructing a third Wuhan fab, ~2027, with half of output pivoting from NAND to DRAM — a major strategic shift toward memory self-sufficiency
- The timeline: both target 2027 production, but market-moving scale — enough to actually pressure Samsung/SK Hynix pricing — doesn't arrive until 2028
- Until then: the big three face no competitive pressure to lower prices. They can ride the crisis. And they know it.
- The 2028 scenario: if Chinese expansion, Samsung new capacity, and SK Hynix HBM ramp all deliver simultaneously, prices could crash. The current $400 price may look like the ceiling, not the floor, in retrospect.
- Sources: [[CXMT and YMTC to Expand Memory Output — Tom's Hardware]], wiki entity pages for CXMT, YMTC
