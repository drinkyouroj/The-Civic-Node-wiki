### Section 1: The Spark — NYT Accuses. The SEC Confirms. (~400 words)
*Tone: direct, specific, slightly sardonic — the irony of Back's situation is load-bearing here*

- April 2026: NYT investigative reporter John Carreyrou, after a two-hour interview in El Salvador, names Adam Back — cryptographer, CEO of Blockstream, inventor of Hashcash — as his primary candidate for Satoshi Nakamoto. Back denied it six-plus times without budging.
- The stylometric analysis (Florian Cafiero, commissioned by NYT): of 12 suspects, Back's writing style matched the whitepaper most closely. Cafiero called it "inconclusive." Back called it a "parlor trick."
- The denials are happening at a specific moment: Back is simultaneously taking a Bitcoin treasury company public via a Cantor Fitzgerald shell company — a transaction requiring SEC registration.
- SEC material disclosure rule: if Back controls Satoshi's estimated 1.1M BTC (~$118B at current price), that is material information for investors in a company he leads. This isn't a philosophical question. It's a regulatory one.
- The August 2008 emails (produced in Craig Wright's London fraud trial): messages between "Satoshi" and Back three months before the whitepaper was published. Carreyrou floats the possibility they were self-sent as cover. He has no evidence for this; the emails themselves are the primary document.
- End with: "The SEC doesn't care who Satoshi is. But it absolutely cares if the CEO of a publicly listed company controls $118 billion in undisclosed assets."

### Section 2: The Pattern — Why Satoshi Was Anonymous in the First Place (~500 words)
*Tone: analytical, building — this is where the Cypherpunk history earns the argument*

- Satoshi's anonymity was not a brand decision. It was engineering. The Cypherpunks understood that a named inventor = a target: tax liability, government compulsion, assassination risk, the "founder sold" market panic. The anonymity was structural to the project's survival.
- The Cypherpunks mailing list is the actual origin story of Bitcoin. Back invented Hashcash in 1997 — a proof-of-work computational puzzle. Satoshi cited it in Section 4 of the Bitcoin whitepaper. Back was present in the exact intellectual community where Bitcoin's ideas were developed.
- The silence gap: records of who was active on the Cryptography mailing list during Satoshi's active period (2008-2010) show anomalous patterns for someone as vocal as Back was before and after. This is not proof; it's the shape of the evidence.
- The 67 hyphenation patterns: not a parlor trick — stylometric quirks (specific compound words, specific punctuation habits) that appear in both Back's documented writing and the whitepaper. Cafiero's "inconclusive" doesn't mean "not him" — it means forensic linguistics can't get you past reasonable doubt on its own.
- The other candidates: Hal Finney (deceased, second-closest match), Nick Szabo (Bit Gold inventor, also denies). The field of serious suspects is small. The Craig Wright fraud trial established that Wright is definitively not Satoshi and produced documents that narrowed the field.
- The design insight: Satoshi built a system intended to outlast its creator's identity. If Back is Satoshi, the design worked for 17 years. Bitcoin didn't need Satoshi after 2010. The question of who built it has always been separate from the question of whether it works.
- Sources: [[Adam Back]], [[Satoshi Nakamoto]], [[NYT Names Adam Back as Satoshi Nakamoto — Bitcoin.com Coverage]], [[Cypherpunk Movement]]

### Section 3: The Protocol — How a Stock Offering Becomes a Confession (~400 words)
*Tone: forward-looking, mechanically precise — this is the section Marcus will forward*

- The SEC disclosure mechanism: the Exchange Act requires disclosure of material information. A CEO who controls ~$118B in unreported Bitcoin holdings that could move markets is not a footnote question — it's the answer to whether the offering document is fraudulent.
- The practical process: every investment bank, underwriter, and securities lawyer involved in the SPAC process conducts due diligence on the CEO's financial interests. There are no off-the-record Bitcoin wallets in that process. You can deny it in an interview; you cannot deny it under penalty of securities fraud.
- The irony — and this is the close of the section: Satoshi built a system designed to escape financial surveillance, government compulsion, and the demand for identity in financial systems. If Back is Satoshi, he is voluntarily entering the most disclosure-intensive process in American capital markets (a SEC-registered public offering) to monetize the ecosystem that system built. The cypherpunk who built money to be free of the state may have chosen a stock offering as his exit.
- What happens at revelation (if it comes): the "founder sold" narrative, the price impact of 1.1M BTC moving from unknown to known ownership, the mythology questions (does Bitcoin survive knowing it was one person?). The network doesn't care. The mythology will.
- For Marcus: the financial system didn't need to hack Bitcoin to find the seam. It just waited for someone to try to go public.
- Sources: [[Adam Back]], [[Blockstream]]

### Section 4: Personal Code — The Inventor Who Built to Disappear (~250 words)
*Tone: first-person, direct, not confessional — the personal angle is the Cypherpunk project itself*

- Personal reflection: the Cypherpunk project was one of the most ambitious acts of preemptive political engineering in the late 20th century — a group of mathematicians who understood that privacy and financial sovereignty were the same problem, and tried to solve it with code.
- The specific angle: what does it mean that the person who may have done the most remarkable thing in the history of money could not take credit for it — and chose not to, deliberately, as part of the design?
- Close question: if the SEC filing produces the answer, what changes? Not Bitcoin. Not the blockchain. Not the mathematics. But maybe the story we told ourselves about why it mattered.

- **Analogy:** Open with a patent filing — the moment an invention crosses from the inventor's notebook into the public record, the inventor loses control of the idea but gains a name attached to it. Satoshi was a patent filing that never cleared the clerk's desk. Then narrow: the SEC is essentially a patent office for financial instruments, and Back just filed.
- **Thesis lands by:** paragraph 2 — "Adam Back is trying to take a Bitcoin company public. If he is Satoshi Nakamoto, he is about to discover that the United States securities disclosure regime does not recognize the Cypherpunk exception."

### Close Strategy
- **Callback:** return to the "patent that never cleared the desk" — except now the desk has a securities lawyer at it
- **Reader leaves with:** the question isn't whether Bitcoin survives knowing who built it. The question is whether the answer matters more to the market or to the mythology — and which one the SEC cares about.
