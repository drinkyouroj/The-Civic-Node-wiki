# The Strait Is the Mandate
## Detailed Outline — Triple Connection Template

**Trigger:** Unexpected Connection — the Strait closure has been covered almost entirely as a
geopolitics story. This piece treats it as the most significant monetary policy event of Q1 2026.
The connection has not been made in this form anywhere in the wiki's source record.

**Template:** Triple Connection
- Section 1 (The Spark, ~400 words): The rate decision nobody voted on

## OPENER APPROACH MAP

**Two viable opener strategies — choose one:**

**Option A — The same building, two votes, one morning:**
On April 29, 2026, two votes happened in the same building complex. At 10am, the Senate Banking
Committee voted 13-11 to advance Kevin Warsh as the next Fed Chair. At 2pm, the Federal Open
Market Committee — still chaired by Jerome Powell — voted to hold rates at 3.50%–3.75% for the
[Nth] consecutive meeting. The old chair was setting the rate the new chair would inherit, in a
building where his successor was already being confirmed. Neither vote moved the price of oil.

**Option B — The thermostat and the open door:**
The broad thing: a thermostat can regulate room temperature. The specific thing: the Fed regulates
the cost of money (and thereby, demand-pull inflation). The bridging property: both regulate one
variable within a system that has other variables they can't touch. The narrow thing: in April
2026, someone left the door open — specifically a door 16 miles wide in the Persian Gulf — and
the thermostat has been irrelevant ever since. What's sitting between the reader and the thesis:
the assumption that inflation is primarily a monetary phenomenon the Fed can control.

**Recommended option:** Option A — it's more specific, dateable, and gives Marcus a concrete hook
he can verify. The 10am/2pm detail (same building, different votes, neither moving oil) is the
kind of structural irony the voice executes well. Option B is in reserve as the closing callback.

**¶1 — what the opening image establishes:**
The April 29 simultaneity. The committee voting on the next chair while the current chair runs
the meeting. The rate decision itself: hold, again. Establish that we are in the present, not
retrospective — the Strait is still closed as you read this.

**¶2 — where the thesis lands:**
The rate that Powell held — and Warsh will inherit — was set in a world where the Federal
Reserve's tools cannot reach the thing causing most of the inflation. Name the Strait explicitly.
Thesis: The dual mandate has a third variable nobody on the FOMC gets to vote on. It's been
running US monetary policy from the outside since February 28.

**Marcus 30-second test:** Does he keep reading? Yes — the 10am/2pm same-building hook is
specific enough to be verifiable and surprising enough to not be obvious. The "nobody on the FOMC
gets to vote on" line is the kind of compressed provocation that earns the next 30 seconds.

**What to avoid:**
- Don't open with the gasoline price. It's the right fact but the wrong entry point — it signals
  "inflation story" before it can signal "monetary policy framing story."
- Don't throat-clear with "The Federal Reserve has long been responsible for..." — start inside
  the event, not the institutional background.
- Don't frame it as an Iran story in the opener. The Strait is infrastructure, not geopolitics,
  for the purposes of this piece.

## Section 2 — The Pattern: "The Tool That Doesn't Fit"
Word target: ~500 words | Tone: analytical, building — this is the densest section
Argument job: Make the structural case that this is not a failure of the Fed's judgment — it
is a categorical mismatch between the Fed's instrument and the problem. Rate hikes address
demand-pull inflation. They do not reopen shipping lanes. This has always been true; 2026 is
just the clearest example.

---

**¶1 — Define the instrument and its intended target**

Facts to deploy (in order):
- The federal funds rate is designed to address demand-pull inflation (too much money chasing
  goods) and inflation expectations (embedded wage-price spirals)
  Source: [[War-Driven Inflation]] — definition section
- Powell, 2025: "Essentially all inflation forecasts for the United States went up materially
  as a consequence of the tariffs" — he said this about tariffs; the mechanism is identical
  for Strait-driven energy prices
  Source: [[Tariff-Driven Inflation]] / [[Trump's tariffs kept Fed from cutting rates, Jerome Powell says]]
- Supply-shock inflation (tariffs, embargoes, chokepoints) does not respond to rate hikes
  because the cause is physical scarcity, not excess demand
  Source: [[War-Driven Inflation]] — "Why It Matters" section

Argument move: Introduces the categorical distinction between demand-pull inflation (rate-tool
works) and supply-shock inflation (rate-tool irrelevant). Does not blame the Fed for this — it's
a design fact, not a failure. This framing matters: the piece is not an indictment of the Fed,
it is a map of the Fed's limits.

Key sentence to land: The cleanest one-sentence statement of the categorical mismatch. Something
like: you cannot raise interest rates to reopen a shipping lane.

---

**¶2 — Historical precedent: 1973 as proof of concept, not just context**

Facts to deploy (in order):
- 1973 Arab oil embargo: OPEC embargo → 400% oil price spike → 1970s stagflation
  Source: [[War-Driven Inflation]] — "Evidence & Examples" section
- Key distinction: Nixon did not cause OPEC. The supply shock was entirely exogenous.
  Source: [[Fed Independence]] — "historical analog" section
- Volcker's rates peaked at ~22% in mid-1981 — but what broke inflation was the oil price
  decline of the early 1980s, not the rate hikes alone. Rate hikes caused the recession that
  reduced demand; they did not restore supply.
  Source: [[Fed Independence]] — Volcker section

Argument move: Uses 1973 not as mood-setting history but as empirical proof of the mechanism.
The 1970s case demonstrates that even extreme rate hikes (22%) took years to work against
supply-shock inflation — and the eventual resolution came from the supply side, not the
demand side.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that distinguishes what Volcker actually did from the myth.
He broke expectations and demand — he did not break the oil shock. That took oil prices falling.
This seeds the implication: the only "tool" that fixes the Strait is a diplomatic or military
resolution, not a rate decision.

Type: Historical parallel — the 1973 / 2026 side-by-side
Location: Transition between ¶2 and ¶3
Suggestion: A short structural contrast: what was true in 1973, what's different in 2026. The

didn't cause OPEC; the US-Israel strikes triggered the Strait closure. The contrast does two
things: (a) confirms the mechanism via historical proof, (b) sets up the "2026 is worse" move

Why it might work: It gives the historical parallel a pointed landing rather than letting it
drift into "here's the historical context" mode.
Why it might not: If the transition is too abrupt, it may read as telegraphing the next section
too obviously.

Facts to deploy (in order):
- Iran is charging ~$2M per ship for coordinated Strait transit
  Source: Bloomberg March 24, 2026 — https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-24/iran-charges-some-ships-hormuz-transit-fees-for-safe-passage

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names the yuan/stablecoin detail as a monetary policy
observation, not a curiosity. Every ship paying Iran in yuan is a small increment of oil-trade
moving off-dollar. The Fed sets the federal funds rate. It does not set the yuan exchange rate.

## Section 3 — The Pattern (cont.): "Two Shocks, One Committee"
Word target: ~300 words | Tone: analytical, building weight — the no-analog argument
Argument job: Establish that the Strait shock did not arrive in isolation — it arrived on top of
an existing supply-shock problem the Fed was already unable to solve. This section makes the
"no clean historical analog" case that Section 4 (Warsh) depends on.

---

**¶1 — Shock 1: Tariffs (endogenous, Trump-removable)**

Facts to deploy (in order):
- Tariff rate: 19.7% effective tariff rate (post-substitution) — highest since 1933, Smoot-Hawley era
  Source: [[State of U.S. Tariffs July 14, 2025]] (Yale Budget Lab)
- Note: use the post-substitution rate (19.7%/1933) for publication; the pre-substitution rate
  (20.6%/1910) is a different metric with a different historical comparison
- Powell: "Essentially all inflation forecasts went up materially as a consequence of the tariffs"
  Source: [[Tariff-Driven Inflation]]
- Key characteristic: endogenous. Same president demanding rate cuts is generating the inflation
  that justifies holding.
  Source: [[Fed Independence]] — "2025 supply shock is endogenous" section

Argument move: Names the first shock and its resolution path (executive action — Trump can remove
it). This is important because it sets up the contrast with the Strait shock: different
origin, different resolution path, same FOMC has to address both with one instrument.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names the political structure of the tariff shock. The
president who created this inflation can also remove it. The Fed cannot.

---

**¶2 — Shock 2: Strait (partially exogenous, Iran's hands now)**

Facts to deploy (in order):
- Cause: US-Israel strikes February 28 triggered Iran's closure
  Source: [[Strait of Hormuz]] / [[Trump threatens hell on Iran infrastructure if Strait remains blocked]]
- Resolution path: diplomatic agreement, military resolution, or indefinite disruption — not
  rate policy. US mine-clearing estimated at 6 months even after political resolution.
  Source: Al Jazeera April 28 — https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2026/4/28/when-will-strait-of-hormuz-be-safe-for-commercial-shipping-again
- Iran submitted 10-point proposal April 28; Trump reviewing, wants nuclear addressed first
  Source: Al Jazeera April 28 (same source)
- Even a diplomatic breakthrough doesn't immediately restore traffic — insurer underwriting
  requires a durable political resolution, not just a ceasefire announcement
  Source: Al Jazeera April 28 (same source)

Argument move: Names the second shock and its resolution path (not in the president's hands;
not in the Fed's hands). The Strait's resolution timeline is measured in months regardless of
what either body decides.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names the resolution asymmetry. The tariff shock is
one executive order away from resolution. The Strait shock has a 6-month floor even after
a deal. These are not equivalent problems wearing the same inflation costume.

---

**¶3 — No clean historical analog**

Facts to deploy (in order):
- 1973: Nixon did not cause OPEC (exogenous supply shock)
- 2026: US strikes triggered Strait closure; Trump tariffs are endogenous; both at once
- The 1951 Truman-Fed standoff is the closest structural precedent: a wartime fiscal president
  demanding accommodation from a Fed that refused. But Truman was not also manufacturing the
  inflation he was demanding the Fed accommodate.
  Source: [[1951 Treasury-Fed Accord]] / [[Fed Independence]]
- "No clean historical analog" finding
  Source: [[Fed Independence]] — "no clean analog" section

Argument move: Names the configuration as structurally novel — not to dramatize it, but to
explain why the historical precedents that usually anchor monetary policy analysis don't map
cleanly here. Warsh will walk into a FOMC meeting without a good playbook.

Key sentence to land: The turn that delivers Section 4. The FOMC operating without a clean
historical analog is not an observation about the past — it's a job description for the
incoming chair.

Type: Structural move — the analogy inversion
Location: Transition from Section 3 to Section 4
Suggestion: The historical frame in ¶3 notes what 2026 is NOT (1973, 1951). The inversion move
