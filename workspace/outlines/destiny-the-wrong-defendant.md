### _"The Wrong Defendant"_

---

Here's the framing that ties the series together: this isn't a story about a streamer's sex scandal. It's a story about **how the legal system assigns blame to whoever is most reachable, not whoever is actually responsible** — and how digital infrastructure (Discord, Google Drive, cloud storage) creates a new class of harm where accountability is structurally impossible to locate correctly.

That's Marcus's hook. He's already suspicious of institutions. He already knows platforms don't absorb consequences — people do. This makes it real and specific.

### PIECE 1: _"You Can't Sue the Catfish"_

**Template**: Template 2 — The System Audit **Timing**: Publish now. The continuance motion hearing on April 21 makes this timely — Pixie is already trying to delay.

**The argument**: The legal system requires a defendant who is reachable. Solo is in the UK, anonymous, and judgment-proof. So the lawsuit landed on the next most-visible person — Destiny — even though Destiny's actual culpability is contested. This is the structural story.

**Section 1 — The Glitch (~400 words)** Open with the moment on November 29, 2024: 26 videos drop on KiwiFarms. Within hours, they're everywhere. Within weeks, a lawsuit is filed — not against the person who posted them, but against someone who shared a video privately in 2022 with a person he believed he knew. The person who actually caused the harm is in the UK and has no name that's legally actionable. So the case goes to the next target in line.

Introduce the three-layer structure cleanly: Rose (identity stolen), Destiny (catfished), the legal record (now twisted to fit a case that doesn't map onto the actual events). Don't editorialize yet — just lay the facts with sourcing.

**Section 2 — The Source Code (~500 words)** _Why does the system work this way?_

Two structural causes: (1) Non-consensual intimate image laws presuppose a direct relationship between the person who shared and the person who was harmed. When a third-party catfisher mediates every interaction, the statute's entire framework collapses — it was written for an ex posting nudes, not for a UK-based identity thief who coerced a teenager and used her face to run a years-long deception. (2) Jurisdictional reality: civil litigation in US federal court cannot touch someone who appears to be Ben Conway in Oxford. The only available defendant is the one with a Miami Beach address and a YouTube channel.

Here's where Rose's deposition lands with maximum force — she testified she had never communicated with Destiny, never controlled any of the accounts in her name, didn't know who Pixie was. The case's entire factual premise dissolves under oath. And the lawsuit continues anyway. That's not a malfunction. That's the system working exactly as designed: go after who you can reach.

**Section 3 — The Upgrade (~400 words)** _What should change?_

Three things: (1) Intimate image statutes need a third-party perpetrator provision — right now there's no path for someone like Destiny (deceived, not the proximate cause of public harm) or Rose (whose identity was weaponized). (2) UK-US legal cooperation on digital identity abuse is essentially non-existent for civil matters. The IIPA has no equivalent extraterritorial reach. (3) Platform design: Google Drive and Discord both facilitated this — Destiny stored content in Google's infrastructure, shared a link with a "trusted" contact, and had no mechanism to revoke access when that contact turned out to be a bad actor. Zero liability flows to the platforms. All of it lands on individuals.

**Section 4 — Personal Code (~200-250 words)** This is where Justin's personal voice enters. The DePIN angle is natural here — the whole critique of centralized cloud infrastructure is that it fails the people who depend on it while insulating the platform from consequences. "I run a node. I think about where my data lives. And the Destiny case is what happens when the answer to that question is 'Google's servers, accessible to anyone I've trusted with a link.'" Keep it brief, sardonic, grounded.

### PIECE 2: _"The Trial That Keeps Not Happening"_

**Template**: Template 1 — The Triple Connection **Timing**: Publish ~May 10-14, the week before the trial

**The argument**: Pixie's motion to continue the trial (filed April 8) is the case's own logic eating itself. The litigation-as-attrition strategy depends on the case never resolving. Every continuance, every delayed deposition, every extended discovery window is the point — not a detour from it. This piece traces that pattern historically.

**Section 1 — The Spark (~400 words)** The April 21 continuance hearing. Pixie's counsel was allowed to attend remotely — but muted. Can't make arguments. The court's posture on this motion is visible in how it structured the hearing. Meanwhile, Destiny's MSJ is pending. If the judge grants summary judgment, the trial never happens. If she denies it, the trial is May 18 — or whenever Pixie can get it delayed to.

Frame the key question for Marcus: why would a plaintiff with a weak case want more time? Because delay is the strategy. The lawsuit's value isn't in the verdict — it's in the cost, the distraction, the reputational shadow it casts for the duration.

**Section 2 — The Pattern (~500 words)** _Historical parallels in litigation-as-attrition._

This isn't new. SLAPP suits (Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation) have been used for decades against journalists, activists, and critics precisely because the cost of defense — not the likelihood of loss — is the weapon. Cite 2-3 specific cases: the record industry RIAA suits against individual file-sharers (not about winning, about creating fear), patent trolling (filing suits not to win but to extract settlements), and recent creator-economy precedents where similar dynamics played out (this is an emerging area). Show the historical pattern without belaboring it.

The Destiny case has a specific wrinkle: the discovery abuse documented in his December 2025 stream. Joan Peters representing at least four subpoenaed witnesses, the false "minor" footnote later retracted, the motion to seal Rose's deposition after it devastated their case. These aren't incidental — they're the litigation-as-attrition playbook executed in real time.

**Section 3 — The Protocol (~400 words)** _What this means for creators, platforms, and law._

The creator economy has no institutional buffer between talent and litigation. When a traditional media company faces a lawsuit, there's a legal team, insurance, institutional memory. A solo streamer — even one with Destiny's audience — is absorbing all of this personally. The asymmetry is structural. And it creates a chilling effect: the lesson other streamers take from watching this case isn't "don't share private content" (the stated purpose of intimate image law). It's "parasocial relationships are litigation risk." That's a distortion the law didn't intend.

Brief note on the second lawsuit (Grace Thorp v. Bonnell, defamation, filed August 2025 in Miami-Dade) — Destiny is now fighting on two simultaneous fronts, which compounds the attrition effect.

**Section 4 — Personal Code (~200-250 words)** Justin's stake: watching powerful legal machinery used not to achieve justice but to inflict cost. This connects to the newsletter's broader skepticism about institutions — courts, like central banks, have mechanics that serve the stated purpose in textbook cases and betray it in edge cases. The edge cases are the honest test.

### PIECE 3: _"The Verdict Doesn't Matter"_

**Template**: Template 2 — The System Audit (revisited, synthesis version) **Timing**: Publish within 5-7 days of resolution — whether trial verdict, summary judgment ruling, settlement, or dismissal

**The argument**: Whatever happens on May 18 (or whenever this resolves), the structural problems that produced the case remain intact. Solo is still in the UK. The IIPA still has no third-party provision. Google Drive still bears zero liability. And 15 other women reportedly received explicit images from Destiny — their stories haven't been told at all. The verdict answers the legal question. It doesn't answer the systemic one.

**Section 1 — The Glitch (~400 words)** Recap the outcome neutrally and quickly — this isn't a verdict recap, it's the setup for the systemic argument. Whatever happened in that Miami courtroom, two things are true: (1) Solo was never in the room and never will be. (2) Rose's name and face were dragged through a federal lawsuit about events she had nothing to do with.

**Section 2 — The Source Code (~500 words)** _What the case revealed about the infrastructure of digital harm._

The case is a case study in four simultaneous accountability gaps: the jurisdictional gap (UK perpetrator, US victims and courts), the platform gap (Google Drive / Discord took zero liability), the statute gap (IIPA written for direct-relationship scenarios, not catfish-mediated ones), and the institutional gap (attorneys who took questionable ethical positions — the false "minor" footnote, witness representation to block depositions — and will face, at most, a bar complaint). These aren't bugs. They're load-bearing features of how digital harm law currently works.

**Section 3 — The Upgrade (~400 words)** _What the next version looks like._

This is the forward-looking section. Three concrete proposals: (1) A third-party perpetrator provision in IIPA or state equivalents — specific to cases where the proximate cause of harm is a catfisher or identity thief who mediated all interactions. (2) Platform liability reform for shared storage — when Google Drive is the vector for intimate image distribution, the "neutral platform" defense should have limits. (3) International coordination: the UK's Online Safety Act has provisions that could theoretically reach Solo's conduct — but US-UK civil cooperation for this class of harm essentially doesn't exist. Name the gap, note what would need to change.

**Section 4 — Personal Code (~200-250 words)** Where this lands for the newsletter's thesis: the Destiny case is a small, specific, creator-economy version of a much larger pattern — infrastructure failures that cost individuals everything while the systems themselves absorb nothing. The Fed can print its way out of a policy mistake. Google takes zero liability for a shared Drive link that destroys someone's life. The asymmetry is the same asymmetry the newsletter covers in monetary policy, in DePIN, in state power. It just has a different name here.

### Series-level notes for execution

**What makes this series pass the Marcus test across all three pieces:**

1. It's not a hot take on whether Destiny is guilty. That's the bait. The hook is structural.
2. It uses primary sources aggressively — Destiny's own transcript reading court filings, Rose's deposition, the PacerMonitor docket. Marcus reads footnotes. These footnotes check out.
3. It builds: Piece 1 gives him the framework, Piece 2 shows the framework in motion, Piece 3 synthesizes. He doesn't need to have followed the case to track the argument.
4. The DePIN/infrastructure angle in the personal code sections isn't forced — Google Drive as "infrastructure you trusted and that failed you" is genuinely continuous with the newsletter's technology skepticism.
5. The series ends with Piece 3 making an argument that **doesn't depend on the verdict**. That's what converts Marcus — a writer who isn't reacting, who anticipated the systemic story before the verdict came in.

**Timing flexibility:** If the April 21 continuance hearing produces something interesting (continuance granted, MSJ ruled on, case settled), Piece 1 can absorb that as a news hook rather than a disruption. The structural argument is verdict-agnostic.

**One adjustment if the case settles before May 18:** Piece 2 and Piece 3 collapse into a single piece titled something like _"Settled. Nothing Changed."_ — the attrition strategy worked, the systemic problems remain, same argument.
