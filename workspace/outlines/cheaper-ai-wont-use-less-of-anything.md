### Section 2: The Mechanics — Coal, Highways, Refrigerators, and the One Question That Determines Everything
Word target: ~600 words | Tone: precise, patient, building
Argument job: Make the mechanism legible through concrete examples, then introduce the key variable (elasticity) that determines when Jevons applies and when it doesn't.

**¶4 — The mechanism in plain language**

Facts to deploy:
- Efficiency lowers the cost per unit of output
- Lower cost makes the thing accessible to more people and more use cases
- New demand can exceed the original savings
- The technical term economists use: "rebound effect" for the general case; "Jevons Paradox" reserved for the extreme case where rebound exceeds 100% (efficiency actually increases total consumption)
  Source: [[Why the AI world is suddenly obsessed with a 160-year-old economics paradox]]

Argument move: Introduces the mechanism cleanly. No examples yet — just the logic.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names the mechanism simply enough that a non-economist can hold it — efficiency → lower cost → new demand → more consumption.

**¶5 — Example 1: Highways (induced demand)**

Facts to deploy:
- Adding lanes to reduce congestion is the classic infrastructure version
- More capacity → lower time-cost of driving → more people drive → congestion returns
- Transportation economists call this "induced demand"
  Source: [[Jevons Paradox]] (wiki concept page)

Argument move: First concrete example. Short, punchy, immediately recognizable.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that collapses the intuition — more lanes didn't fix traffic because the "fix" created the traffic.

💡 [RHETORICAL SUGGESTION — USE OR SKIP]
Type: Compression / parallel
Location: Closing of ¶5
Suggestion: A one-sentence structural echo connecting highways to coal — "Jevons saw it with coal. City planners see it with lanes. The resource changes; the pattern doesn't."
Why it might work: Compresses two examples into a rhythm that feels like discovery. Marcus likes pattern recognition delivered dry.
Why it might not: Could feel too tidy this early — the pattern should still be building, not yet confirmed.

**¶6 — Example 2: Refrigeration and A/C (David Owen / New Yorker)**

Facts to deploy (lead with the number, then the household scene):
- **Killer stat (lead with this):** Between 1993 and 2005, the average new A/C unit became ~28% more efficient. Household A/C energy consumption rose ~37% over the same period. The efficiency gain and the consumption gain run on parallel tracks.
  Source: [[The Efficiency Dilemma — David Owen New Yorker 2010]]
- Refrigeration: The 35-year period during which new refrigerators plunged in electricity use is also the period during which the global refrigeration market burgeoned
- Owen's family archetype: old fridge moved to basement, stayed plugged in 25 years, joined by stand-alone freezer and bar icemaker. Modern kitchens: side-by-side fridge + side-by-side freezer + under-counter beverage mini-fridge
- Gas stations now have almost as much refrigerated shelf space as 1960s grocery stores
- Second-order: since mid-1970s, per-capita food waste in US increased by half (40% of edible food discarded) — refrigeration conveyed false sense food would last longer
  Source: [[The Efficiency Dilemma — David Owen New Yorker 2010]]

Argument move: Second example, more surprising than highways. The A/C stat does the heavy lifting — a single sourced number proves the mechanism before the prose describes it. Escalates from infrastructure (highways) to consumer goods (fridges/A/C) — bringing the pattern closer to the reader's kitchen. Food waste is a second-order Jevons effect that makes the concept deeper than expected.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that makes the concept vivid — the efficient fridge didn't replace the old one, it multiplied the *idea* of refrigeration. The A/C number proves it in advance so the sentence lands as confirmation, not assertion.

**¶7 — Example 3: Video streaming (bandwidth)**

Facts to deploy:
- Before modern compression, 360p video used significant bandwidth
- Better codecs didn't reduce bandwidth consumption — people streamed more content, at higher resolutions, on more devices simultaneously
- Total bandwidth consumption exploded
  Source: [[Big A — The Crisis Got Weirder (RAM Apocalypse Update)]]

Argument move: Third example, digital and recent. Bridges from physical (highways, fridges) to digital — setting up the AI pivot.

Key sentence to land: Brief — two sentences max. The bandwidth example should land fast because the pattern is now established.

**¶8 — The counterexample that breaks the pattern: agriculture**

Facts to deploy:
- Tractors made farmers enormously more productive
- But food demand is **inelastic** — people don't eat 6x more when food gets 6x cheaper
- Farming went from 40% of the US workforce (1900) to under 2% today
- Efficiency destroyed millions of jobs because demand couldn't grow fast enough to absorb the productivity gains
  Source: [[Why the AI world is suddenly obsessed with a 160-year-old economics paradox]]

Argument move: **This is the turn.** Everything before this paragraph said "efficiency increases consumption." This paragraph says "unless demand is inelastic" — and the counterexample is devastating (40% → 2%). The argument suddenly has stakes.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that names the variable — "food demand is inelastic" — and lets the reader feel the implication before it's stated.

**¶9 — The one question: elastic or inelastic?**

Facts to deploy:
- Three conditions required for a labor-market Jevons effect: (1) the technology makes workers more productive, (2) higher productivity translates to lower prices for the output, (3) demand must be elastic enough to grow in response to lower prices
  Source: [[Why the AI world is suddenly obsessed with a 160-year-old economics paradox]]
- The single variable: Is demand for the thing being made efficient elastic or inelastic?
- If elastic → Jevons applies → efficiency increases consumption
- If inelastic → efficiency destroys jobs and reduces consumption

Argument move: Delivers the diagnostic tool the piece promised. This is the "precision gift" — the one question Marcus can carry out of the article and apply to every efficiency claim he encounters.

Key sentence to land: The sentence that states the question plainly enough to be memorable and portable: "Is demand for this elastic or inelastic?"
