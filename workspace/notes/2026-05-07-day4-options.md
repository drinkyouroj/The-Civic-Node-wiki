### Option A — Iran's IRGC permit regime (Bloomberg, April 1)

Bloomberg confirmed in April that Iran's IRGC is running a structured permit system for Strait of Hormuz transits: contact an intermediary, submit ship data, pass a security screen, pay in Chinese yuan or stablecoins, receive an escort.

The payment architecture is the detail that matters. Every transit fee is denominated off-dollar. The Strait is now both a supply chokepoint and a persistent dedollarization mechanism running on live oil-linked trade.

The Fed's rate-setting tools don't reach either function. Tomorrow's issue goes into what that means for the next FOMC meeting.

*Source: [Bloomberg, April 1 — "Secret Codes and Yuan Fees Get Ships Through Iran's Hormuz Tollbooth"](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-01/strait-of-hormuz-ships-paying-iran-yuan-and-crypto-tolls-for-safe-passage)*

### Option B — Warsh 13-11 committee vote (Senate Banking, April 29)

The Senate Banking Committee voted 13-11 on April 29 to advance Kevin Warsh as Fed Chair. Strict party line.

Every prior Fed Chair confirmation produced at least some cross-party votes at the committee level. This one produced zero. The institutional norm that kept the Fed Chair outside partisan arithmetic just ended at the committee.

The floor vote follows the week of May 11.

*Source: Senate Banking Committee roll call, April 29, 2026*

### Option C — IMO Secretary General quote + traffic data

"There is no safe transit anywhere in the Strait of Hormuz." — IMO Secretary General, April 2026.

That's the official assessment from the intergovernmental body that coordinates global shipping safety. The week of April 13-19, about 80 vessels moved through. Pre-war daily average: 130-plus.

The inflation number reflects this. The FOMC's next statement will not mention it.

## NOTE 2: Contested Claim

---

### Option A — The chair is the wrong protagonist

Every piece of Fed coverage this week is organized around Kevin Warsh. His credibility, his Senate vote, his first press conference.

The Strait of Hormuz is the binding constraint on US inflation right now. The incoming Fed chair will hold one of twelve FOMC votes. He can't vote to reopen a shipping lane.

The counterargument is that the chair shapes committee communication, which shapes expectations, which matters. True. The problem: inflation expectations don't move oil tankers. Tomorrow's issue is built around that gap.

---

### Option B — The Strait is being covered as a military story

The Strait of Hormuz coverage is organized around ships and weapons. The finance layer is getting missed.

Iran is running a toll road with a navy. Every vessel pays up to $2 million, in Chinese yuan or stablecoins, for an IRGC escort. The revenues compound regardless of how the diplomatic track resolves.

The counterargument is that the fees stop when the conflict ends. The problem: the yuan-denominated payment infrastructure doesn't.

---

### Option C — The rate cut debate is asking the wrong question

Before the next FOMC meeting, one question is worth asking: will the Fed's statement describe what's actually driving inflation?

Two supply shocks are running simultaneously — the Strait closure and a 19.7% effective tariff rate, highest since 1933. Rate policy reaches neither. The committee's statement will either name that constraint clearly or obscure it.

The counterargument is that central bank communications always simplify. The problem: this time the simplification has a measurable cost.
