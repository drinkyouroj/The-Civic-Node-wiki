## LINKEDIN REPOST — post after the flagship is live (~8:00 PM ET)

*Professional register, every figure cited to its source, no first-person, no CTA, ends with the link. ~370 words.*

In December 2025, the Department of Homeland Security announced Operation Metro Surge as the largest immigration enforcement operation in U.S. history, deploying roughly 2,000 federal agents to the Minneapolis–Saint Paul metropolitan area to remove what officials repeatedly described as "the worst of the worst."

Six months on, three institutions have documented in detail what the operation actually involved: the city's budget office, the federal courts, and the public-records process.

The economic record. Minneapolis's updated impact assessment, released June 10, 2026, estimates close to $700 million in lost wages and business closures across the December 2025–April 2026 window (MPR News; Star Tribune; CBS Minnesota). A separate amended federal complaint from Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison's office, drawing on a U.S. Immigration Policy Center survey of 1,390 residents and roughly 900 businesses, pleads approximately $610 million in lost business revenue and $243 million in lost wages metro-wide.

The arrest record. Federal arrest data obtained through a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit by the Deportation Data Project, analyzed by MPR News/APM Reports and the Minnesota Reformer, documents at least 3,789 arrests during the operation. Of those, the department publicly named 335 individuals, roughly 9 percent, as illustrative of the "worst of the worst"; in the full dataset, 63 percent of those arrested had no criminal record of any kind, and 97 percent of arrests were non-custodial street encounters rather than removals from existing custody. Because ICE's definition of a "crime" includes misdemeanors such as unauthorized entry and traffic violations, even the minority with a conviction overstates serious criminality. Roughly 35 percent of arrests were "collateral," meaning the individuals were not targets. A Minnesota Reformer review of more than 1,100 habeas corpus petitions identified 273 people holding valid work permits, including asylum seekers, refugees, and DACA recipients.

The judicial record. Chief U.S. District Judge Patrick Schiltz documented ICE violating 96 court orders across 74 cases by January, and an additional 113 by late February, a total of more than 200 violations across roughly 150 cases. He threatened ICE's acting director with contempt to secure one detainee's release and wrote that he was "not aware of another occasion in the history of the United States" in which a federal court had to repeatedly threaten contempt to compel the government to comply with its orders. In a separate matter, after an ICE agent killed U.S. citizen Renée Good, the FBI took exclusive custody of the physical evidence and excluded state investigators.

The December announcement and the documented record describe two different operations. The agency published roughly 9 percent of the arrest record; the remainder surfaced through litigation. The full analysis:
