SESSION: 2026-06-08
TOPIC/STORY: Moving from Seattle (Capitol Hill) back to suburban Detroit after the 2024 election — relocating to "blend in" during the Trump term, and watching Operation Metro Surge confirm the fear that drove the move.

NARRATIVE SUMMARY:
Justin watched the 2024 election as someone who believed he already knew the outcome. He says he "knew it was coming" — but the foresight didn't insulate him from the fear; if anything it sharpened it. When Trump won, the intellectual prediction collapsed into something bodily: he "freaked out and got scared." The specific fear was geographic and political — that a second Trump administration would move against the blue West Coast, and that Washington state, where he was living, would be a target for retribution. His response was to leave. He moved back to Michigan, a purple state, explicitly to "blend in."

The fear was not abstract paranoia. He frames Operation Metro Surge — the federal enforcement occupation of Minnesota — as the thing that proved him right. What he describes as genuinely unreal to him was not enforcement per se but the form it took: "a US President using the national guard and branches of the military to occupy states." He had imagined retribution against blue territory; Minnesota became the demonstration that it could actually happen. The emotional core of the session sits in one line: being told he was right to fear, about something he never wanted to be right about. There is no relief in the vindication. The forecast paid out in a currency he didn't want.

The move's logic is the quietly unsettling part, and he states it plainly: he feels safer because he feels less visible. He traded Capitol Hill — a dense, protest-heavy core of Seattle — for a suburb of Detroit, where he is, in his words, objectively less likely to end up in or near a demonstration. He named the result himself: it's boring. But he wanted boring. Boring was the chosen environment for this term, not "excitement." He delivers it with a wry, self-aware acceptance, but underneath the wryness is a citizen who reorganized his physical life around minimizing his own exposure to his government.

EMOTIONAL SUBTEXT:
The dominant undercurrent is the gap between the analyst and the frightened person. His public posture is the forecaster's — "I knew it was coming" — but the verbs that describe the actual decision are fear verbs: "freaked out," "scared," "feared retribution." He holds both at once, and the newsletter voice will be tempted to keep only the analyst. The honest version includes the flight.

The second undercurrent is the reframing of safety as invisibility. He doesn't say the move made him safe; he says it made him feel safer *because* it made him less visible. That's a charged thing to say matter-of-factly about one's own country — the operating assumption that the path to safety is to be unseen by the state. He's not performing alarm about it; he's already metabolized it into a logistics decision.

There may be an unnamed grief in the trade. Capitol Hill is not a neutral place to leave — it is Seattle's protest heart and historically its queer center. He framed the move entirely in safety/visibility terms ("objectively less likely to be involved in demonstrations"), and called the result "boring." Whether "boring" is pure relief or also a quiet loss of a place with identity and energy, he didn't say — and I won't assume. But the word choice ("excitement" in scare-quotes, as the thing he was rejecting) suggests he's defending the choice against an internal objection he didn't voice.
