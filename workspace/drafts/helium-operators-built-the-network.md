# Helium Operators Built the Network. Nova Labs Sets the Price.

## A 2025 governance proposal authorized one company to negotiate carrier pricing without a community vote. Half the yes votes were carried by that company's proxy and the co-author's.

A business and a franchise are not the same thing. When you buy a business, you set the prices, choose your suppliers, and decide who to do business with. When you buy a franchise, you bought the right to use someone else's brand on terms the franchisor sets through a contract you signed. The truck, the storefront, the equipment are yours. The pricing power is not.

Helium hotspot operators bought a franchise. They were told they bought a business.

They built the network: [385,000 hotspots](https://messari.io/report/state-of-helium-q4-2025), partnerships with T-Mobile and AT&T, $22 million in annualized revenue at year-end 2025. But the governance proposal that finalized who sets carrier pricing, HIP-143, [passed 90% to 9%](https://heliumvote.com/hnt/proposals/4EemLuZhTgtv6SFNogMByCTEx4DFtBKc32qEhDKL9iaZ) on April 3, 2025. Half the yes votes were carried by the proposing entity's proxy and one of its co-authors. The hotspot owners weren't in the room when the rules were written. They just paid the electricity bill.

## The Glitch: How the same network stopped paying the people who built it

The first thing an operator notices is the math.

A basic indoor IoT hotspot costs $249. A pro outdoor setup runs to $949. Marketing once promised hundreds of dollars a month. By August 2025, a well-placed urban hotspot was [earning between $3 and $45 a month](https://eng.ambcrypto.com/how-much-can-you-really-earn-with-helium-hotspots-in-2025/), with most operators clustered in the $4 to $8 range. The indoor box pays back in two and a half to five years; the outdoor setup, ten to twenty.

The token price moved with broader crypto markets, but the deeper change was governance.

[HIP-82](https://github.com/helium/HIP/blob/main/0082-helium-mobile-service-provider.md) capped how much data could earn rewards: $0.50 per gigabyte up to whatever the subscriber's plan cost divided by $0.50 allows, zero above that. [HIP-138](https://github.com/helium/HIP/blob/main/0138-return-to-hnt.md) consolidated the IOT and MOBILE subnetwork tokens into HNT in January 2025. The August 2025 halving cut new HNT emissions from 15 million to 7.5 million per year, [halving Proof of Coverage rewards in the same step](https://blog.helium.com/helium-halving-2025-8ecaa1fff464). Each proposal had its own justification. Together they shifted the rewards from coverage to data traffic, then halved what could be earned on the coverage that remained.

If your hotspot sits in a city block dense with other hotspots, the rewards split more ways. If it sits alone in a suburb, the [Lone Wolf rule](https://eng.ambcrypto.com/how-much-can-you-really-earn-with-helium-hotspots-in-2025/) cuts you out of Proof of Coverage entirely, because the algorithm requires a neighboring hotspot within LoRaWAN range. The structure that pays you exists only where you compete with another operator for the same scarce data.

Who made those decisions, and did the operators have real input?

## The Source Code: A vote carried by the entity it authorized

The official rationale for each change was defensible on its own.

Coverage was gameable. The [PoC hacking case](https://hntnews.org/poc-hacking-explained/) was documented in detail by community researchers: bad actors clustered miners at false locations and had them witness each other's beacons over distances the radios couldn't actually cover. Rewarding data transfer instead of coverage put incentives on a metric closer to actual utility. Caps on rewardable data stopped operators from earning indefinitely on subscribers who'd already maxed out their plan. Token consolidation simplified the accounting.

Each step had a defensible logic. Run them in sequence over three years and you get a different network.

Coverage value spreads out. Data transfer value concentrates in dense urban corridors near Helium Mobile subscribers. An operator who built out a rural hex in 2022 created a real public good for the network's IoT ambitions. They were later told it wasn't the public good that pays.

As of Q4 2025, the IoT half of the network generates [$124.77 per day](https://messari.io/report/state-of-helium-q4-2025) in Data Credit burns across the entire deployed base. About $45,000 a year. Roughly the price of one Helium Mobile Air subscription per day, generated by the 385,000 hotspots that operators built to serve IoT traffic. The mobile side, which routes carrier offload from T-Mobile and AT&T, generates the rest: [$56,635 per day](https://messari.io/report/state-of-helium-q4-2025) in real utility revenue. The economics of the two halves of the network diverge.

The final structural change came in April 2025. [HIP-143](https://github.com/helium/HIP/blob/main/0143-decoupling-service-provider-pricing-from-governance.md) authorized Nova Labs (the company that operates Helium Mobile and negotiates the carrier deals) to set the price carriers pay for hotspot data without involving Helium governance. The justification appears in the proposal text itself: "The pricing of mobile offload agreements is complex and often confidential. If Nova Labs would be able to move quickly...without involving Helium governance, the authors of this HIP anticipate that the total Data Transfer of the Helium network will rise."

That sentence describes the architecture. The DAO is the hardware deployment mechanism. Nova Labs is the commercial counterparty. The operator is the link that connects hardware ownership to commercial value. They aren't in the room when the commercial terms are set.

The vote that passed HIP-143 looked like community consensus: 90.53% in favor, 763 million veHNT cast against a 100 million quorum. Look at the [voter breakdown](https://heliumvote.com/hnt/proposals/4EemLuZhTgtv6SFNogMByCTEx4DFtBKc32qEhDKL9iaZ) and the picture changes. The Nova Labs proxy controlled 26.00% of the vote. The ferebee proxy, listed as a co-author of HIP-143, controlled 24.00%. Together, the proposing entity and one of its co-authors cast half the yes votes for the proposal authorizing Nova Labs's pricing authority. The largest "against" vote was an anonymous proxy at 8%.

The Helium Foundation's own [explainer on the August 2025 halving](https://blog.helium.com/helium-halving-2025-8ecaa1fff464) recommends operators "set a proxy as a backup to ensure you don't miss out on rewards." Foundation policy normalizes delegating voting power to large proxies. The concentration that approved HIP-143 is how the system was designed to work.

## The Upgrade: What honest operator economics would look like

Decentralized governance of commercial relationships is hard. The engineering constraints are real.

Carriers negotiating offload pricing don't want to put the terms on chain. Helium Mobile's pricing leverage with T-Mobile depends partly on confidentiality, since AT&T and Movistar can see the same public chain. Nova Labs's argument that fast, confidential negotiation can't happen through public veHNT voting is structurally correct. A DAO that wants to behave like a commercial counterparty has to do this in some way.

The gap between what the marketing said and what the governance documents reveal is what makes the structure feel like a trap.

A network sold as "earn tokens by providing wireless coverage" is, in structural terms, "provide capital for infrastructure in exchange for token rewards that one company is authorized to renegotiate." Both descriptions can be true. Operators bought based on the first one and discovered, through a series of HIPs, that they had agreed to the second.

What honest operator economics would look like, if anyone were building it:

A clear floor on what triggers a material change to the reward structure, with operator right to exit when that floor is crossed. Operators who deploy under coverage-pays would have a notice period and a buyout option when coverage stops paying.

Aggregate transparency on revenue from data transfer agreements. Carrier-by-carrier disclosures aren't necessary if confidentiality matters; the network as a whole disclosing what it earns per gigabyte across its commercial book is sufficient for operators to evaluate their own position.

Explicit acknowledgment that rural operators are providing a different good (coverage) than urban operators (traffic), with separate pricing that reflects the distinction. The current architecture treats both as commodity hardware in the same reward pool, then quietly stops paying one of them.

Disclosed upfront. Before deployment, not nine months after.

The honest version of the DePIN franchise is still a real product. "You're providing capital for infrastructure in exchange for a revenue share, priced by a centralized negotiator, on hardware you own" is a defensible offer. People buy revenue-share positions all the time. They tend to want to read the contract first.

## My Debug: The counter-example I cited was a rug

Two earlier pieces in this newsletter pointed to [Datagram](https://drinkyouroj.substack.com/p/the-depin-scam) as the DePIN project doing it differently. The community sale wasn't structured the same way. The team had learned, the pieces said, from what went wrong with Gala Games. The CEO talked about avoiding the foundation-plus-labs structure.

Datagram turned out to be a rug.

I ran over a dozen Datagram nodes for a few weeks. I worked on the project for about seven months without pay. The project collapsed.

This is the personal proof of the piece's argument. The operator due diligence question (who controls pricing, has governance changed rewards mid-game) wasn't enough to catch what Datagram actually was. I was looking at the structural questions the previous pieces had identified. The question I missed was whether any structure was being built at all.

The honest admission is that the DePIN operator checklist I'd been refining isn't sufficient on its own. Some projects have governance problems. Some projects have pricing opacity. Some projects are just fraud. The structural problems make it harder to tell which is which, because the same documents that warn you about governance theater can also describe a legitimate but constrained project.

The food truck franchise analogy assumed the franchisor was at least running a real restaurant business. Not all of them are. The Helium operator can read the franchise agreement and at least know what they bought. The Datagram operator was reading a brochure for a restaurant that never opened.

The structural problems and the fraud problems share an architecture. Both require an operator who deploys capital before the documents that govern what they bought are even written. The right move, given that, might be to never deploy first.
