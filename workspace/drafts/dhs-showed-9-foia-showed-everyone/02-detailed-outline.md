**¶3 — The courts' ceiling**

Facts to deploy (in order):
- Civil contempt vs. criminal contempt — the two enforcement tools Schiltz has
  Source: [[Juan T.R. v. Noem — Schiltz Orders on ICE Court-Order Violations]]
- Civil contempt: coercive, meant to compel compliance (daily fines); not pardonable by the president
  Source: [[Juan T.R. v. Noem — Schiltz Orders on ICE Court-Order Violations]]
- Criminal contempt: punitive, meant to punish defiance; IS pardonable — the Joe Arpaio precedent (2017: Trump pardoned Arpaio before his contempt sentencing)
  Source: [[Juan T.R. v. Noem — Schiltz Orders on ICE Court-Order Violations]]
- Enforcement mechanism for court orders: ultimately runs through U.S. Marshals, who answer to the executive
- The ceiling: the judiciary can document defiance and impose costs — it cannot unilaterally end federal enforcement operations. That enforcement depends on the executive branch to carry out.

Argument move: Names the courts' tools and their limits. The civil/criminal distinction is the analytical key — one is pardon-proof and coercive; the other is punitive but pardonable. Together they explain why Schiltz's criminal-contempt threat is less powerful than it sounds.

Grounding plan: Grounds in "Arpaio precedent" (named actor, named event, dated: 2017), "U.S. Marshals" (named institution), "daily fines" (concrete mechanism). The abstract claim ("the judiciary can't unilaterally end this") grounds in the Marshals dependency — a named institutional fact, not a political assertion.

Statistic framing: No statistics. The argument is about mechanism, not magnitudes. The Arpaio pardon is the only "number" (2017) and it's a date, not a figure — it functions as a historical anchor, not a statistic.

Key sentence to land: The pardon-distinction sentence — civil contempt is coercive and not pardonable; criminal contempt is punitive and is. These aren't two versions of the same tool; they're different tools with opposite pardon exposure. Keep them separate and specific.
