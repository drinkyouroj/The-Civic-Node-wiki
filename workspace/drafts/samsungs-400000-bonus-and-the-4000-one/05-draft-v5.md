# Samsung's $400,000 Bonus, and the $4,000 One

## Inside one company, the AI windfall split 100 to 1. Outside it, the same boom lands on a power bill nobody voted on.

Two workers leave the same Samsung plant in Pyeongtaek at the end of the same shift. They worked it a hundred yards apart. One builds memory chips; this year his bonus runs toward [$400,000](https://archive.ph/jshgC). The other builds phones and televisions; his comes to about [$4,000](https://archive.ph/jshgC). Same company. Same shift. A hundred to one.

That gap is the deal Samsung's chip workers [ratified on May 27](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10756822): [10.5% of their division's profit, paid out every year for a decade](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/samsung-chip-workers-vote-to-accept-340000-average-bonus-ending-months-long-strike-threat). They won it at the one spot in the AI economy where the people doing the work had a hand on the valve. Bloomberg calls the winners the ["AI aristocrats."](https://archive.ph/jshgC) The chip worker got his cut because he could choke the supply the whole boom depends on. Most people are standing somewhere with no valve to grab.

The leverage was real, and it was measurable. Samsung alone is [22.8% of South Korea's exports and 26% of its stock market](https://archive.ph/N83CM). A walkout in Pyeongtaek doesn't dent one company's quarter; it stalls the AI buildout worldwide, because the high-bandwidth memory those workers make (the stacked chips an AI accelerator can't run without) is sold out. Samsung's profit had jumped 755% year over year. And they had a template: SK Hynix's union had pried loose [10% of operating profit](https://en.sedaily.com/markets/2026/05/05/sk-hynix-faces-100-trillion-won-burden-for-shareholder) [the previous fall](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-04/sk-hynix-set-to-pay-2-7-billion-bonuses-to-quell-labor-tensions), so the number was already on the table before Samsung's workers asked for it. They were sitting on the one input nobody could substitute, in the one quarter it mattered most. So they asked for a cut, and they got it.

The win didn't land evenly, and Samsung's own people said so. The smaller union, the one for the phone and appliance side, approved the deal at just [21%](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10757190); thousands quit Samsung's biggest union during the talks rather than bless a bonus they were mostly cut out of. Another union swelled from about 2,600 members to 13,000 and started preparing a legal challenge. Shareholders are suing too, arguing a profit share written into a labor contract may break Korea's commercial law. The aristocrats got their cut. Everyone else got a memo about feeling "alienated."

Which leaves the question the headlines skipped. If the people at the chokepoint captured a decade of the upside, the cost of this boom didn't disappear. It went looking for somewhere with less resistance.

---

## The Pattern: The same boom, pointing the other way

Follow the electricity instead of the silicon and you arrive at the other chokepoint. Every data center needs a large transformer (the equipment that steps power up and down to move it across the grid) before it can switch on, and the wait for a high-capacity unit has [stretched to four years](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/05/11/u-s-transformer-market-faces-severe-supply-constraints-as-lead-times-extend-to-four-years/). Demand for them is up [more than 100% since 2019](https://www.powermag.com/transformers-in-2026-shortage-scramble-or-self-inflicted-crisis/) while the factories that build them barely grew, which is how a wait of two and a half years became four. The gate behind that gate is stranger: the steel a transformer's core needs, grain-oriented electrical steel, comes from [exactly one mill in the United States](https://www.clevelandcliffs.com/operations/steelmaking/butler-works), Cleveland-Cliffs, in Butler, Pennsylvania. One factory's output paces the entire domestic supply.

Cleveland-Cliffs' CEO puts the dependency about as plainly as it goes: ["there will be no AI without electricity, and there will be no electricity without transformers."](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/cleveland-cliffs-confirms-150-million-electric-transformer-weirton-plant/723363/) The grid is a chokepoint exactly like the memory fab. The difference is that nobody here has a hand on the valve.

And the cost is already arriving. PJM, the grid operator for 13 eastern states, ran its capacity auctions (where the grid pays generators to guarantee future supply), and the math came back ugly: data-center demand added [$13.77 billion to customer bills](https://www.monitoringanalytics.com/reports/PJM_State_of_the_Market/2026/2026q1-som-pjm.pdf) across two annual auctions, with wholesale power up 75.5% year over year. Those higher bills land on households in Ohio and Virginia, who never voted on the data centers driving the number and now pay more for the same electricity.

So it's one boom hitting two chokepoints, and the outcomes run in opposite directions. At the memory chokepoint, a workforce that could organize and could stop the line took a share of the upside. At the grid chokepoint, the people nearest the cost can't organize the queue or the weather, so they absorb it. What connects the two stories is the cause they share. The Samsung worker's bonus and your power bill both come out of this boom. But his raise didn't pay for your bill, and your bill didn't fund his raise.

The thread joining them isn't money moving from one to the other. It's leverage. In both places the boom hit a bottleneck and asked the same question: who here can say no? The chip workers had a union and a product nobody could source elsewhere, so they could. The ratepayer has a billing address. So he can't.

## The Protocol: Labor wrote a claim, the grid got a bill

What the Samsung workers actually won is worth being precise about. Not a raise. A written claim on 10.5% of the division's profit, renewing every year for a decade. Thin on paper, structural in effect. The last time a one-paragraph document quietly rewired who controlled a surplus, it was the [1951 Treasury-Fed Accord](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/treasury-fed-accord), the agreement that freed the Federal Reserve from propping up wartime bond prices. It happened because the side with the weaker hand refused to keep accommodating the stronger one until the stronger one signed. A thin instrument, won by refusal, can move a surplus for decades. That's the company this contract keeps.

Here's the part that complicates the easy version of the story. Because the bonus is a slice of profit, not a charge stamped on every chip, it [doesn't sit on the cost of production](https://www.investing.com/analysis/samsungs-strike-risk-is-gone-now-the-real-test-is-hbm-200680992) the way a wage hike would. It comes out of what's left after the chips sell. SK Hynix signed its version first; its combined shareholder and worker payouts [jumped about 450%](https://en.sedaily.com/markets/2026/05/05/sk-hynix-faces-100-trillion-won-burden-for-shareholder) this year while spending on plants and research grew 20 to 30%.

So the windfall lands on shareholders and on the budget for the next factory. It eats the reinvestment that keeps a chokepoint a chokepoint.

Could Samsung claw that margin back by charging more for memory? It could. It sells into a near-monopoly with its fastest chips sold out, and JPMorgan figures the labor deal alone is a [7-to-12% drag](https://fortune.com/2026/05/17/labor-strike-samsung-ai-hbm-chips-dividend-revolution-memory/) on this year's operating profit, a number big enough to tempt any pricing department. But "could" is the honest verb. Nobody has shown Samsung raising chip prices to fund the bonus pool, and memory prices are climbing this year mostly because Nvidia can't get enough, not because a union voted. If the cost ever reaches the cloud giants buying the chips, it'll be because Samsung chose to use its leverage, not because a wage bill rolled downhill on its own.

The claim is already climbing past the factory floor. Samsung and SK Hynix together could owe [more in corporate tax than Seoul expected from every company in the country combined](https://archive.ph/jshgC) this year, and that single fact has turned a labor story into a national one. Korea's presidential policy chief is floating a "citizen dividend" funded by AI tax revenue, calling it a chance to become "the first country to return the excess profits of the AI era to human life." The labor minister who brokered the truce admits his government has "no precedent to follow." They're improvising a way to share a windfall that's outrunning the institutions built to tax it.

And the playbook travels. Within days of the Samsung vote, TSMC, the one chipmaker whose leverage rivals the Koreans', told its own staff their profit-sharing would [rise more than 30%](https://archive.ph/jshgC). In the US, [Axios reports](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/25/ai-profit-revolt-samsung-california-socialism) the "AI productivity dividend" is shaping up as a 2026 proxy-season demand, with the UAW and CWA studying what Samsung's union just pulled off. So the same question travels with it. At the chokepoint you can organize, labor wrote itself a claim on the windfall. At the chokepoint you can't, the bill just shows up.

## Personal Code: I read the wrong variable

I called this one wrong in April. In ["The Bluff Is Over. The Price Isn't."](https://drinkyouroj.substack.com/p/the-bluff-is-over-the-price-isnt) I flagged the chip workers as a supply risk and watched for a walkout that would choke the memory market. Right chokepoint, wrong variable. They didn't walk out; they signed. The strike was the threat. The contract was the point. I'd been watching the threat instead of the thing the threat was for.

The part of this I actually feel isn't the bonus. It's the line on my own power bill, which is creeping up for reasons that have more to do with a data center two counties over than with anything I plugged in. I'm not the $400,000 worker in this story. Odds are you aren't either.

So picture the two workers leaving that plant again, a hundred yards and a hundredfold apart. By the end they stand for the two ends of this boom: one on a chokepoint with a union behind him, one beside the cost with neither. The $4,000 worker at least got something. The ratepayer is that worker with no union and no vote. Nobody has tried to organize his side of it yet.
