# The Accord That Wasn't Signed

*The Federal Reserve's most consequential 2026 decision won't be a rate cut. It will be the balance sheet — and the Treasury Secretary already has a vote.*

---

In 1951, the Federal Reserve won its independence from the Treasury in a fight it held in public. A sitting president summoned the entire rate-setting committee to the White House and demanded it keep his borrowing costs low. The committee said no, in writing, and that refusal became the agreement that has governed the central bank for seventy-five years. You can still go read it. It is a document, with a date and signatures, sitting where anyone can find it.

On Wednesday, Kevin Warsh ran his first meeting as Fed chair, and the unwinding of that arrangement arrives with no such document. There is no memo to leak, no vote to lose, nothing to request under the Freedom of Information Act. Wall Street spent the afternoon parsing whether the rate would move. The rate decision is the alibi. The decision that matters is the one the statement was rewritten to stop making.

---

## The problem: three pieces, one picture

Three news stories arrived in three separate weeks. Read them as one institutional sequence and the picture changes.

The first piece is the rough draft. Stephen Miran held a Federal Reserve Governor seat *and* the White House Council of Economic Advisers chair on unpaid leave from September 2025 through February 3, 2026, when he [resigned the CEA chair](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/03/feds-stephen-miran-resigns-from-white-house-post.html) under sustained pressure from Sen. Warren and others. During the unpaid-leave period he had no White House email or badge and committed to providing "no advisory guidance as part of CEA in any way." The procedural firewall was the official answer. The structural bridge was the actual arrangement. His FOMC voting record while seated: five consecutive dissents, every meeting he attended, every one of them in the direction of more cuts than the Powell majority wanted. The March 2026 FOMC vote was 11–1 to hold. Miran was the 1. The dual-role experiment was the visible, clumsy version. What followed is the elegant one.

The second piece is the public alignment. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been aligning openly with Kevin Warsh's balance-sheet framework since at least February. [TradingKey reported on February 9](https://www.tradingkey.com/analysis/economic/central-banks/261567407-kevin-warsh-fed-bessent-qt-rate-cut-treasury-tradingkey) that Bessent stated the Fed would not rapidly reduce its balance sheet, directly anticipating what Warsh would later propose at his hearing. On April 25, Bessent told reporters that ["if the Fed wanted to wait for some clarity before cutting rates, I understand that."](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/04/25/dont-count-on-rate-cuts-just-yet-warsh-as-fed-chair-may-not-lead-to-big-policy-changes/) Beat reporters wrote it up as restraint. It is the opposite. It is Treasury establishing that its preferred Fed Chair has political cover to hold, which is the most durable form of coordination because it doesn't look like coordination.

The third piece is the institutional proposal. At his [April 21 confirmation hearing](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-04-22/warsh-wants-fed-to-back-bessent-rubio-statecraft-agenda-for-the-dollar), Warsh proposed a formal new Treasury-Fed accord that would coordinate Fed balance-sheet runoff with Treasury debt-issuance timing. He called the post-2008 Fed balance sheet ["fiscal policy in disguise."](https://www.capitalflowsresearch.com/p/decoding-the-warsh-testimony-what) His preferred posture, in his own words, is "not subordinate, but more coordinated." His preferred description of the institution itself: "independent inside of government, not independent of government." Most coverage treated his testimony as a confirmation pitch. It was a policy spec.

Three news stories. One institutional sequence. The dual-role experiment that made the bridge visible, the Treasury Secretary aligning publicly with the framework, the chair-in-waiting writing the framework into testimony. Independence isn't being attacked. It's being renegotiated by the people it's supposed to constrain.

---

## The analysis: what a "new accord" actually would be

The reason "more coordinated" is the operative phrase is what it is displacing.

The [March 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/treasury-fed-accord) ended the Federal Reserve's wartime obligation to peg Treasury bond yields and restored its authority to set monetary policy independently of Treasury debt-management needs. The Accord was won over Harry Truman's direct objection. He summoned the entire FOMC to the White House (the only time a sitting president has done so) to demand the peg be held; the Fed refused. After the Accord, the Fed could raise rates without Treasury permission and the Treasury had to issue debt at market-clearing yields. Before it, neither was true. Modern Fed independence is 75 years old, not constitutional, and it began as a refusal.

What Warsh is proposing has two [operational commitments](https://jinlow.substack.com/p/the-warsh-proposal-fed-treasury-coordination): explicit forward Fed guidance on the balance-sheet trajectory, and Treasury alignment of debt-issuance timing with Fed liquidity operations. Krishna Guha at Evercore ISI [called it](https://www.tradingkey.com/analysis/economic/central-banks/261567407-kevin-warsh-fed-bessent-qt-rate-cut-treasury-tradingkey) "veto power over quantitative tightening decisions." Tim Duy at SGH Macro called it Yield Curve Control by another name, "explicitly connecting monetary policy to deficit financing rather than inflation management." Deutsche Bank modeled the operational consequence: the Fed's holdings of short-term Treasury bills would shift from less than 5% of the portfolio to roughly 55%. That is not a tactical adjustment. It is a structural transformation of what the central bank is. Three credentialed desks, three vocabularies, one mechanism: the Treasury Secretary becomes a non-voting member of the FOMC in everything but name.

The motive is fiscal arithmetic. Federal net interest costs hit [$270.3 billion in Q1 FY2026](https://jinlow.substack.com/p/the-warsh-proposal-fed-treasury-coordination), about $1.08 trillion annualized on $38.56 trillion of total federal debt. The Fed balance sheet is at $6.6 trillion. Treasury wants the Fed coordinating balance-sheet decisions because debt service is now a trillion-dollar-per-year line item, and any sustained increase in borrowing costs is politically intolerable. The Treasury has a fiscal problem that the Fed currently has the discretion to make worse. The accord proposal removes the discretion. That is the policy. Coordinated rates are downstream.

There is one more name in [Bloomberg's headline](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-04-22/warsh-wants-fed-to-back-bessent-rubio-statecraft-agenda-for-the-dollar): Marco Rubio. Bloomberg framed Warsh's Fed agenda as backing a Bessent-Rubio "Statecraft" agenda for the dollar. [Capital Flows Research](https://www.capitalflowsresearch.com/p/decoding-the-warsh-testimony-what) called the unstated objective an "FX endgame": coordinated dollar weakening to restore US trade competitiveness without losing reserve-currency status. That is interpretation, not reported fact. No leaked memo names dollar weakening as a coordinated objective, and the AP wire [explicitly notes](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/04/25/dont-count-on-rate-cuts-just-yet-warsh-as-fed-chair-may-not-lead-to-big-policy-changes/) that "no explicit coordination between Bessent, Miran, and Warsh is documented." But three independent desk reads (Evercore, SGH Macro, Deutsche Bank) converging on the same operational structure is a market-consensus signal worth registering. The institutional facts are damning enough without the conspiracy.

[An earlier piece here](https://drinkyouroj.substack.com/p/independent-inside-of-government) reframed Powell-Trump as Martin-LBJ rather than Burns-Nixon. The 1951 Accord was institutional, not personal — the Fed's leadership refused as a body, then institutionalized the refusal. What Warsh is proposing inverts the move. He is not capitulating under pressure, the way Burns did in the seventies. He is walking in with the capitulation pre-drafted as policy architecture. The 1951 Accord institutionalized a refusal. The Warsh framework would institutionalize the agreement.

## The implications: the Bessent cover, the hidden vote

When the Treasury Secretary says he "understands" if the Fed wants to wait, he is not exercising restraint. He is establishing that his preferred chair can hold rates without it being read as caving to Trump pressure. That gives Warsh runway. Runway buys time for the structural moves: the balance-sheet changes that don't generate the same political optics as a rate cut but reshape the Fed's portfolio, its independence posture, and its operational coordination with Treasury debt management. Wall Street is currently [pricing minimal probability](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/04/25/dont-count-on-rate-cuts-just-yet-warsh-as-fed-chair-may-not-lead-to-big-policy-changes/) of rate cuts before October 2027. Jon Faust, a former Powell adviser, told the AP that "Warsh comes in with essentially none of the gravitas that Greenspan had. Instead, Warsh comes in with the baggage that Trump has really loaded on him." That is the rate-decision constraint. The market is watching the constraint. The market is watching the wrong instrument.

Twelve people sit on the FOMC. Warsh holds one vote, and the chair-as-test-case is Miran losing 11–1 in March. But the balance sheet is a different category of decision. It is less granular, less publicly contested, less covered by the financial press, and structurally negotiable with the Treasury Secretary outside the FOMC framework entirely. Treasury is not on the FOMC. Under Warsh's proposal, Treasury does not need to be. The thirteenth seat doesn't appear on any organizational chart, and that is the point.

Independence, post-1951, is the Fed's ability to make decisions the Treasury Secretary doesn't agree with. Coordination, as Warsh defines it, is the Fed agreeing in advance not to. That is not a description of independence. It is a description of what a 1951 reversal would look like, written in language too careful to call itself one.

## The June FOMC tell

Warsh ran his first meeting on June 17. The test was never whether he'd cut or hold. It was what the statement would say — and what it would stop saying.

The committee held the rate at 3.5 to 3.75 percent and voted 12 to 0. No dissent, after a March meeting that split 11 to 1. The projections flipped hard: the median official now pencils in a quarter-point *hike* this year, where in March the median saw a cut. That hawkish turn is what the financial press led with, and it is the loudest alibi the rate decision has produced yet. A chair installed by a president demanding cuts, presiding over a committee that just signaled a hike, reads as independence on its face. So read the rest of the same statement.

It was cut to roughly 130 words, down from over 300. The forward guidance — the sentence that signals where policy is headed — was deleted; Warsh's reason was that it is "not well suited for the current policy conjuncture." The balance sheet, the instrument this whole piece is about, was reduced to a single line: the committee "reaffirmed its policy of maintaining ample reserves." Warsh declined to submit his own projection to the dot plot — eighteen of nineteen officials did, and the chair was the one who didn't. And the actual redesign of what the Fed holds and how it lines up with the Treasury's debt calendar was moved into newly announced task forces, which issue no statement, take no FOMC vote, and leave nothing to FOIA.

The tell was never going to be *in* the balance-sheet paragraph. The tell is that the balance-sheet paragraph is now one sentence, the guidance is gone, the chair's own forecast is off the record, and the part that decides the institution's future has been relocated to a room with no transcript. You were told to read the document. The new chair's first act was to write a shorter one.

---

In 1951, the Federal Reserve refused a wartime president and won an institution. In 2026, the chair walking in has the refusal already withdrawn — not in a memo, not in a vote, but in the language of the proposal itself. Independence began as a no. The Warsh framework is a yes, narrated as coordination. The rate decision is the alibi. The balance sheet is the policy. The accord is already operative; the signing was always the optional part.
