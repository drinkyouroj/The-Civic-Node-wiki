I Had the Wrong Protagonist

*Process note — analytical backstage for [The Strait Is the Mandate](https://drinkyouroj.substack.com/p/the-strait-is-the-mandate)*

I wrote two Fed pieces in two weeks treating the chair as the protagonist. That was the wrong protagonist.

The first was about the Powell pressure campaign. The second was about the Warsh confirmation. Both organized around the same question: who holds the seat, and what their public record implies about direction.

The analysis reorganized when I started looking at what rate policy can actually reach.

Rate policy reaches demand-pull inflation. A wage-price spiral, domestic demand running hot. That's the condition rate hikes address. Volcker's record is real. But what ended the 1970s inflation was OPEC normalizing supply and demand destruction running its course. Volcker hiked into the shock. The shock resolved through different mechanisms. The Fed gets credited for a sequence it was only partly responsible for. Using Volcker as the template for a supply-shock environment requires demand-pull inflation. That requirement isn't in the data right now.

The Strait of Hormuz has carried roughly 20% of global oil supply. Through April, roughly 95% of pre-war traffic stopped transiting. The figure I kept returning to: 191 tracked crossings in April against a pre-war pace of roughly 3,000 per month. At a certain point the percentage becomes a tanker count and you stop reading it as a macroeconomic concept. When you see 191 where 3,000 should be, the supply shock stops being a model variable and becomes a logistics problem with no monetary-policy dimension. Rate policy has no pathway to that chart.

The tariff structure is the same category. The effective rate sits at 19.7%, highest since Smoot-Hawley. The FOMC didn't set it. The FOMC can't unset it.

Two binding constraints, neither addressable by rate policy. That's the environment before anyone at the Fed does anything.

The piece I was originally building was organized around Warsh: whether he's independent from the White House, whether his prior record implies a directional shift. Both are real questions. Both are also questions about the chair. The more productive question turned out to be about the mandate.

Warsh inherits those constraints. He holds one of twelve FOMC votes. His credibility arrives pre-loaded with everything the confirmation process required him to say. None of that changes what the instrument is built for.

June 16-17 is Warsh's first meeting as chair. The statement that follows is the document to read. It will either name the two constraints the rate tool can't reach, or it won't. A statement that doesn't name them is a political document regardless of who signed it. Read it for what's missing.

The piece ended up being about the tool. When the private analysis produces a correction, the published work should say so.
