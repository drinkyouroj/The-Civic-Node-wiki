## Summary

The House unanimously passed a 10-day stopgap extension of Section 702 of FISA in the early morning hours of April 17, 2026, after a GOP leadership-negotiated five-year reauthorization deal collapsed on the floor. Twelve Republicans voted with nearly all Democrats to reject the amendment package (200–220). A subsequent procedural vote to advance a clean 18-month reauthorization failed 197–228, with 20 Republicans defying [[Donald Trump]]'s explicit call to pass it "clean." Speaker [[Mike Johnson]] pivoted to the 10-day extension (through April 30) to buy negotiating time.

## Key Points

- Section 702 authority was set to expire April 20, 2026; stopgap extends to April 30
- Trump publicly demanded a clean 18-month reauthorization with no reforms; GOP right-flank (Freedom Caucus plus Intel Committee members) defied him
- 12 Republicans voted against the leadership-negotiated amendment package; 20 voted against the procedural rule
- Four Democrats (Gluesenkamp Perez, Jared Golden, Josh Gottheimer, Tom Suozzi) crossed over to support the rule — unusual procedural defection
- Core dispute: warrant requirement before FBI/DOJ access to 702-collected communications involving Americans
- Democrats (Rep. [[Jim Himes]]) feared the leadership amendment's warrant language "restate[d] current law" while opening the door to DOJ-approved searches with little oversight
- Himes proposal: require FISC judge approval before any US-person query, standard of "reasonably likely to return foreign intelligence information"
- Johnson acknowledged "FISA abuses of the past" language — implicit reference to [[Crossfire Hurricane]]-era criticisms

## Newsletter Angles

- **Coalition Fracture**: This is one of the clearest instances of the House GOP's right flank defeating Trump on a "must-pass" vote — direct evidence that MAGA-aligned skepticism of the surveillance state can overpower presidential direction when paired with privacy-minded Democrats. The right/left civil-liberties coalition is real and occasionally decisive.
- **Constitutional Gymnastics**: The core fight is over whether the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement applies to intelligence-community queries of 702 data on Americans. Leadership's text was crafted ambiguously enough that both sides feared it favored the other — classic transparency theater applied to surveillance reform.
- **Institutional Gaslighting**: The "we fixed FISA abuses" narrative runs alongside refusal to put warrant requirements in statute. If abuses were truly past, why resist the statutory fix? The stopgap is a structural admission that the reform isn't actually wanted.
