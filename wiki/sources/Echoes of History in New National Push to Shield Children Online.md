## Summary
NYT historical feature pairing the 1996 [[Communications Decency Act]] and [[Reno v. ACLU]] (1997) with the 2023 wave of state/federal age-restriction bills. Draws the parallel between the ACLU's original CDA challenge (Chris Hansen, Ann Beeson) and current [[NetChoice]] lawsuits against [[California Age-Appropriate Design Code Act]]. Notes that age verification has gotten much cheaper (as little as $0.10/visitor with driver's license integration) since 1997.

## Key Points
- CDA (1996) criminalized knowingly sending/displaying obscene/indecent content to under-18s online.
- [[ACLU]] sued — needed to be directly threatened, so posted George Carlin's "seven dirty words" transcript plus a quiz.
- [[Reno v. ACLU]] (1997, 9-0): SCOTUS struck down indecency provisions, affirming "you can't censor speech to adults in the name of protecting minors."
- Court's 1997 rationale relied on content-filtering software being available and age verification being rare/expensive (credit-card based).
- 2022 [[California Age-Appropriate Design Code Act]] requires defaults-to-max-privacy for minors; [[NetChoice]] suit pending.
- [[Congressional Research Service]] March 2023 memo warned of unintended consequences.
- [[Brian Schatz]] (D-HI) leading new Senate minimum-age bill.

## Newsletter Angles
- The doctrinal test: if current courts follow [[Reno v. ACLU]] precedent, most of these bills get struck down. If they don't, it signals a major First Amendment shift on age-based speech restrictions. KOSA/SCREEN Act litigation will be a bellwether.
- Ted Cruz anecdote as color: as a SCOTUS clerk, he helped O'Connor look at adult image results to demonstrate internet access — now advocates for the inverse.
- The "cheaper infrastructure" argument: one reason age verification is back is that vendors now claim $0.10/visitor integration. Court's 1997 economic assumptions are inverted.
