## Overview
**ICE** (U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement) is the Department of Homeland Security agency responsible for interior immigration enforcement, detention, and removal. Its Enforcement and Removal Operations (ERO) division is the unit named in the "ERO assist" search tags that recur across the wiki's surveillance sourcing. ICE is a connective entity across three of the wiki's clusters — immigration enforcement, surveillance-infrastructure repurposing, and federal-vs-state accountability — appearing far more often as a *queryer of other systems* than as a directly-sourced subject, which is why it warranted a dedicated page.

## Key Facts
- **Operation Metro Surge:** ICE/CBP ran the Dec 2025–Feb 2026 mass enforcement campaign in Minneapolis — 3,789+ arrests, ~35% collateral (non-target), 96+ documented court-order violations. [[Operation Metro Surge]]
- **Killing of Renée Good:** an ICE agent (Jonathan Ross) fatally shot U.S. citizen Renée Good (Jan 7 2026); DOJ investigated the victim rather than the shooter — a central instance of the [[Federal Immunity Above Constitutional Law]] pattern. [[Killing of Renée Good]]
- **Surveillance-by-proxy:** ICE and sibling agencies query the [[Flock Safety Surveillance Network]] without their own accounts — local agencies run "ERO assist" / immigration-keyword searches on their behalf, so ICE reaches sanctuary-jurisdiction camera data the city's policy forbids it to request directly. [[Atlanta PD used Flock cameras to track migrants]]
- **The vendor workaround:** ICE access is a worked example of [[Vendor-State Governance]] — the agency need not hold a contract or a warrant when a networked third party answers the query. [[The Bill of Rights Ends at the Contractor's Door]]
- **Detention contracts:** cities including Atlanta have terminated ICE detention contracts as a sanctuary measure — a policy-layer action that, the wiki repeatedly shows, does not reach the system-layer (camera networks, federal queries).

## Newsletter Relevance
ICE is where the **Power**, **Politics**, and **Technology-&-State** themes intersect: an enforcement agency whose reach is amplified less by its own infrastructure than by its ability to query *other people's* — local police ALPR networks, vendor databases, sanctuary cities' own cameras. Its accountability gap (a federal agent who kills a citizen and is investigated less than the citizen) and its surveillance reach (querying systems no policy authorized it to touch) are two faces of the same structural story the newsletter tracks.
