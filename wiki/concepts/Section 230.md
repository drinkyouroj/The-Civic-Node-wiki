## Definition
Section 230 is 47 U.S.C. § 230, the federal statute that grants online platforms immunity from civil liability for most user-generated content, and protects platforms' "Good Samaritan" editorial decisions to remove content. Enacted as part of the Communications Decency Act of 1996 and authored by then-Rep. [[Ron Wyden]] and Rep. Chris Cox, it is the single legal provision that makes the modern internet — comment sections, social media, search engines, app stores — structurally possible as a U.S. business model.

## Why It Matters for the Newsletter
Section 230 is the scaffolding under almost every platform-liability fight in this wiki. Each new "child safety" bill — [[Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA)]], [[EARN IT Act]], [[STOP CSAM Act]] — works by carving exceptions into Section 230 or by imposing duties that only make sense if 230's shield is narrowed. Understanding what these bills actually do requires understanding what they're eroding. For Politics/Power coverage, 230 is also a rare bipartisan punching bag: Republicans claim it enables "Big Tech censorship," Democrats claim it enables harm to minors — both framings converge on dismantling the same shield for opposite reasons.

## Evidence & Examples
- **Statutory origin**: Enacted February 8, 1996 as part of the Communications Decency Act, Pub. L. 104-104. Core operative text is 47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1): "No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider" [[Section 230 Legislative History]].
- **Co-author**: Sen. [[Ron Wyden]] (D-OR), who remains its most prominent defender in the Senate.
- **First major carve-out — SESTA/FOSTA (2018)**: Stripped 230 immunity for content facilitating sex trafficking. Widely documented as having pushed sex workers into less safe conditions without reducing trafficking — the template case for "230 carve-outs don't do what they claim" [[Section 230 Legislative History]].
- **KOSA's mechanism**: Creates a duty of care enforceable by state AGs; while KOSA doesn't formally amend 230, the duty-of-care standard operates as a de facto liability regime for design choices, narrowing 230's practical shield [[Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA)]], [[Child safety bills are reshaping the internet for everyone]].
- **EARN IT Act**: Explicit 230 carve-out for CSAM-related state-law claims; EFF and allies argue it would functionally mandate scanning of encrypted content [[Bad Internet Bills]].
- **California algorithmic-liability bill**: State-level attempt to hold platforms liable for algorithmic amplification of hate-fueled content — pushes at 230's edges at the state level [[If Hate-Fueled Algorithms Cause Real-World Harm, California's Tech Companies Should Pay]].

## Tensions & Counterarguments
- **The "26 words" defense**: Advocates (EFF, Wyden, Fight for the Future) argue 230 is load-bearing for all online speech, not just Big Tech — any weakening hurts smaller platforms disproportionately.
- **The accountability critique**: Critics across the political spectrum argue 230 has outgrown its 1996 context; platforms are no longer passive conduits but active amplifiers, and immunity for algorithmic design decisions is different from immunity for hosting user posts.
- **Bipartisan-convergence problem**: Republicans and Democrats want to narrow 230 for incompatible reasons (less moderation vs. more moderation). The resulting legislation tends to satisfy neither goal while expanding enforcement discretion for state AGs and the FTC.
